# Hear the changes with books you already own

You don't need to buy anything to start hearing chord changes. Your library already holds a working chord-hearing routine with tenor/Bb audio. It is spread across five books you own, and nothing in the catalog points to it yet. Every educator in the notes teaches the same order: hear the **chord qualities** (maj7, m7, dom7, m7b5), then follow the **roots in the bass**, then hear the **ii-V-I as one sound**, then move to whole standards from recordings ([Jazzadvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/how-to-hear-chord-changes/); [LJS](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/learning-jazz/ear-training/ear-training-chord-progressions/)). Your owned books cover every step except one: repeated drills with right/wrong answers. Two free browser tools that run on Linux fill that gap. These are **teoria.com** and the **iwasdoingallright Online Ear Trainer**, and both have explicit jazz ii-V-I drills. If you want one purchase, **Aebersold's *Jazz Ear Training*** (about $12–15, with Bb parts) is the cheap guided option. **Radley's *Real Easy Ear Training Book*** ($25–34) is the deeper one, and it is built entirely around hearing chord changes. Paid courses are optional. The best value is **Jazzadvice PRO at $108/yr**, but it has no refunds. No credible source says how long this takes. The educators call it slow and frustrating at first, and say it improves through exposure to the same tunes over and over.

## Your top-pick path in five steps

Following the changes on the Playbook while reading the chart already does half the job. Your eyes give you the answers, so now the ears have to catch up. This path uses only what you own plus free tools:

1. **Hear the chord qualities.** Play Essential Elements for Jazz Ensemble tenor audio tracks **31–35** ("Building Jazz Chords" Maj/Maj7/7/Mi7 and the chord workouts) and **36 "Listen to the Blues Progression"**. These are the only owned recordings that put maj7, dom7 and m7 side by side.
2. **Sing, then play, the roots of *Autumn Leaves*.** Use the Playbook backing track. First sing the bass notes (Playbook pp. 17–18, "Listen to the bass notes"). Then play the roots on tenor, then root + 5th, using *Let's Play Jazz!* Ch. 3's bass-line exercise.
3. **Add guide tones.** Play and sing the 3rds and 7ths from the Playbook's guide-tone charts (pp. 36–37).
4. **Drill with free tools.** On teoria.com, work through *Seventh Chord Ear Training* and then *Jazz Progressions* (II-V-I category) ([teoria](https://www.teoria.com/en/help/revision-history.php)). If teoria gets dull, use the Online Ear Trainer's jazz mode instead ([Online Ear Trainer](https://www.iwasdoingallright.com/tools/ear_training/online/)).
5. **Take it to the band's tunes.** Before you open the chart for a new tune, sing its roots along with a recording. Then check what you heard against the 557 fake book.

The rest of this report backs up each step and covers what to buy if you want more.

## Bass first: why every teacher starts with the roots

The sources disagree on wording but not on order. Learn Jazz Standards calls intervals "the first step you need to take to identify chords by ear". After that come chord qualities, and then progressions, which you find by working out the roots from the bass intervals and then each chord's quality ([LJS](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/learning-jazz/ear-training/ear-training-chord-progressions/)). Jazzadvice is blunter: "the most direct way to figure out a chord progression is to focus on the roots of all the chords" ([Jazzadvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/how-to-hear-chord-changes/)). It also explains why this is hard for you in particular. Horn players' ears go to the top of the sound by habit, so hearing the bass takes deliberate effort. With a walking bass, the roots usually fall on **beats 1 and 3** ([Jazzadvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/how-to-learn-jazz-standards-with-the-piano/)).

Its self-test lays out the ladder: sing the root over a single V7, then over a 12-bar blues backing track, then a jazz blues, then a real record such as Miles Davis's *Trane's Blues* ([Jazzadvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/ear-training-test/)). The common mistakes are memorising chord symbols instead of the sound, starting with reharmonised recordings, and assuming a descending 4th in the bass means I-IV. A descending P4 is the same note movement as an ascending P5, so it can just as well be V-I ([LJS 80](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/ljs-podcast/jazz-tips-and-advice/ljs-80-chord-progressions-identify-ear/)). *Autumn Leaves* is a good place to start because it is almost entirely major and minor ii-V-Is. It works as both the practice piece and the test.

You don't need a piano. LJS says you "don't even need your instrument" for this work ([LJS](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/?p=133649)), and the owned *Standards Workbook* tells single-line players to arpeggiate the shapes. A cheap keyboard or a piano app is still a useful extra for sounding a chord to check a guess. Jazzadvice calls two-note shell voicings at the piano "the most overlooked" way to learn a tune's harmony ([Jazzadvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/how-to-learn-jazz-standards-with-the-piano/)).

Some of what you own does not help with this. The BetterSax ear-training videos (Pentatonic Foundation, Blues Foundation, the Shed "Play by Ear" cheatsheet) are all **melodic**: you copy phrases, you don't identify chords. ## Two free browser tools fill the only real gap

Your books show you the sounds, but they can't quiz you. Drills with right/wrong answers fill that gap, and on Linux the best ones run in the browser. **teoria.com** lets you choose which 7th-chord types to drill. It also has a maintained **Jazz Progressions** exercise, which gained 39 new II-V-I exercises in 2024 and 468 seventh-chord pattern exercises in June 2026 ([teoria](https://www.teoria.com/en/help/revision-history.php)). The **iwasdoingallright Online Ear Trainer** is free and covers all five 7th qualities. Its jazz progression mode includes ii-V7, iii-vi-ii-V-I, turnarounds, the major and minor blues, the Rhythm Changes bridge and the minor ii7b5-V7b9. A Custom tab lets you type in the changes of tunes you're learning ([Online Ear Trainer](https://www.iwasdoingallright.com/tools/ear_training/online/)). One caveat: I couldn't confirm whether every teoria exercise is free.

On a phone, the free **EarMaster** app includes chord identification. Its **Jazz Workshops** bundle is a $14.99 in-app purchase on iOS ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/earmaster-music-theory/id1105030163)), but EarMaster has no Linux version. **Functional Ear Trainer** is free and good for hearing scale degrees in a key, though it doesn't identify chords ([Fifths](https://fifths.io/blog/best-ear-training-apps/)). **Chordify** guesses chords from recordings using AI. It makes mistakes, so use it only to check an answer.

For free listening, the Jazzadvice article "How to Hear Chord Changes" is the clearest one-page method ([Jazzadvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/how-to-hear-chord-changes/)). Open Studio's free podcast episode "How to Hear the Changes" (#22) is also worth a listen ([You'll Hear It](https://youllhearit.com/episodes/how-to-hear-the-changes-22)).

## If you buy one thing: a $15 book beats a $229 course at this stage

**Aebersold *Jazz Ear Training*** is the cheapest guided step. It has about two hours of recorded exercises with spoken instructions, and runs from intervals through 7th-chord qualities, walking bass, half-diminished chords and II-V7-I. All the answers are printed, and parts for C, Bb and Eb are included. It costs **$15**, or $12 for the digital-only version ([jazzbooks.com](https://www.jazzbooks.com/jazz/product/JETCD)). **Radley's *The "Real Easy" Ear Training Book*** is aimed squarely at your goal. The author co-chairs Berklee's ear-training department, and the subtitle is "A Beginning/Intermediate Guide to Hearing the Chord Changes". It starts from bass-root movement and uses 150+ well-known songs, at **$25 digital** ([jazzbooks.com](https://jazzbooks.com/jazz/product/REET)). Berklee's $1,575 OEART-320 course is built on this same book ([Berklee Online](https://online.berklee.edu/courses/harmonic-ear-training-recognizing-chord-progressions)). Leave these for later: Masakowski (Mel Bay) and Donelian Vol. 2 are intermediate to advanced. Coker's *Hearin' the Changes* is a catalogue of progressions with no audio I could confirm.

## Conclusion

Your problem isn't missing material. Your library already covers each stage, from chord sounds to roots to guide tones, but the exercises sit in separate books, and none of them is labelled as ear training. Two free browser drills add the missing quizzes, so spending money is a choice, not a requirement. The cheapest real upgrade is Aebersold's $15 book, not a subscription.

The biggest lever is the material you already play every week. Sing the roots of your band's tunes along with a recording before you look at the chart, and that turns weekly repertoire into ear training. It is the "exposure" that every source credits with progress. Once *Autumn Leaves* sounds like a chain of ii-V-Is without the chart, the same skill carries over to most of the 557.
