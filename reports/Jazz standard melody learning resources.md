# Learn each head from two recordings and one chart

You don't need a new app or book to learn a standard's melody. The best method uses **two well-chosen recordings, a free slow-down tool, and the band's 557 chart as the final word**. Educators agree on how to choose the recordings. Take one *straight* version for the true melody: a singer who sings it as written (Sinatra is the standard example) for Songbook tunes, or the composer's own recording for tunes written by jazz musicians. Then add one simple, clear instrumental version, ideally on tenor, for the sound and feel. The process is the same everywhere too. Listen with the horn put away, sing the melody, find it on the horn phrase by phrase at 75% speed, then check it against the chart. For your band there is one extra final step: **read the head from the 557 page while the recording plays**, because reading and keeping your place is the actual band skill. On Linux a free YouTube slow-down extension covers the recording side. Transcribe! (about €37) is the one desktop app worth buying, and Anytune (about $15) is the equivalent on a Mac or phone. iReal Pro plays no melody, and Aebersold tracks don't either. The only play-along books that *play the head for you* are Hal Leonard's Jazz Play-Along series, with the melody on its own stereo channel, and its Real Book Multi-Tracks. One of those volumes is the most useful thing to buy if a band tune is in it.

## Pick one straight version and one tenor version

The most useful rule in the notes is Brent Vaartstra's: **"Know who to listen to for feeling and who to listen to for accuracy."** Billie Holiday takes many liberties with a melody, while "Frank Sinatra on the other hand almost always sings the melody straight." Miles Davis is a poor source for melody accuracy unless the tune is his own ([Vaartstra, LJS](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/?p=23884)). The LJS podcast divides this by tune type. For Great American Songbook tunes, a straight singer is the reference. For tunes written by jazz musicians, use the original recording by the composer or the composer's band ([LJS Podcast](https://www.everand.com/podcast/709265483/How-Do-I-Know-the-Real-Melody-to-a-Jazz-Standard)). Applied to your band list, that means a straight vocal version for All of Me, Autumn Leaves, Body and Soul, Bye Bye Blackbird, Georgia, Summertime, Stella by Starlight and There Will Never Be Another You. Doxy, Now's the Time, Blue Bossa, Perdido and Satin Doll are learned from the composer's or original band's recording. Jazzadvice adds a second criterion for the first version: it should be **"simple and clear"**, with an audible bass line, a melody played plainly, no reharmonization, and a tempo you can follow. That usually means an older recording ([Jazzadvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/3-tricks-to-learning-tunes-from-recordings-that-have-nothing-to-do-with-practicing/)). BetterSax gives the same advice for beginners: a slow or medium recording you like ([BetterSax](https://bettersax.com/how-to-learn-tunes-without-the-real-book/)).

That makes "a couple of versions" the right number, with a defined job for each. The straight version gives you the notes. A tenor version by Dexter Gordon, Sonny Rollins, Stan Getz, Ben Webster or Lester Young gives you the sound, but expect those players to embellish the head, so don't treat their notes as the melody. Jazzadvice's optional third version is one you love and "listen obsessively" to for its vibe ([Jazzadvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/3-tricks-to-learning-tunes-from-recordings-that-have-nothing-to-do-with-practicing/)). No source names a magic number. Vaartstra's own range goes up to "as many recordings as possible" ([Smart Way PDF](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Learn-Jazz-Standards-the-Smart-Way.pdf)), so two is a floor, not a ceiling.

Two free references find these recordings quickly. **jazzstandards.com** tune pages have a "Getting Started" student pick and a CD list. For Autumn Leaves the student picks are Cannonball Adderley's *Somethin' Else* and Bill Evans' *Portrait in Jazz*, and the list includes Sarah Vaughan as a vocal version. The site looks unmaintained since about 2020–22, but that doesn't matter for classic tunes ([jazzstandards.com](https://www.jazzstandards.com/compositions-0/autumnleaves.htm)). **Ted Gioia's *The Jazz Standards*** covers about 250 tunes with about a dozen recommended versions each (2nd ed. 2021) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Jazz_Standards:_A_Guide_to_the_Repertoire)). Open Culture published a roughly 2,000-track playlist built from its picks ([Open Culture](https://www.openculture.com/2017/05/__trashed.html)). For listening, YouTube and free Spotify are enough. A library card often adds **Naxos Music Library Jazz** (about 250,000 tracks) or Freegal at no cost ([Vancouver PL](https://vpl.ca/digital-library/naxos-music-library-jazz); [Mentor PL](https://mentorpl.org/?p=12848)). Lyrics are worth learning for vocal tunes: Vaartstra calls them "extremely helpful for internalizing melodies" ([Smart Way PDF](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Learn-Jazz-Standards-the-Smart-Way.pdf)). Bebop heads usually have no words, so those are learned from the instrumental recording with a slow-downer.

## Find it by ear, then read it from the page

The earlier tune-learning report set out the general listen–sing–play order. What's new here is how the ear work and the chart work fit together when the goal is reading at band rather than memorizing. The educators split on one point: whether to play along with the record while you find the notes. Vaartstra says no: "You should not be listening to a recording while learning the melody on your instrument!" Sing it first, then find it alone ([LJS](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/?p=23884)). BetterSax's Jay Metcalf says yes. Loop a section at **75% (50% if fast)** and play it "over and over again along with the recording until you can match what you're hearing" ([BetterSax](https://bettersax.com/how-to-learn-tunes-without-the-real-book/)). Both camps agree on short chunks. Metcalf's lesson works in four-bar, then eight-bar phrases, "Don't try to learn the whole song all at once" ([BetterSax](https://bettersax.com/learn-melodies-by-ear-practice-saxophone/)). A workable compromise is to sing the phrase, find it on the horn without the record, and then confirm it by playing along at 75%.

The chart's role depends on the source. For Vaartstra, sheet music checks "the work your ear has done" on ambiguous spots, ideally with the recording playing as you go through it ([Smart Way PDF](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Learn-Jazz-Standards-the-Smart-Way.pdf)). John Cipolla's university handout is the reading-friendly view. Play the melody "exactly as it is written" slowly with the metronome on 2 and 4, "do not embellish it," and keep the music in front of you "until it begins to become second nature" ([Cipolla, WKU](https://people.wku.edu/john.cipolla/Teaching_files/Learning%20Tunes.pdf)). Your band goal sits between the two. The ear stage keeps the melody from sounding generic, which is the lead-sheet-only sound Metcalf warns about. The reading stage trains what band actually asks of you, which is following the 557 page and keeping your place. Fake-book charts reflect one recording's version, sometimes with a different key or an embellished melody ([Jazzadvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/3-tricks-to-learning-tunes-from-recordings-that-have-nothing-to-do-with-practicing/)). When your ear and the 557 disagree on a note, **play the 557 at band**, since that is what everyone else is reading. Mark the spot, and use the recording to decide how to phrase it.

On tenor, one practical issue is register. Tenor sounds a major 9th below written. Reading a concert chart, you go up a whole step and add two sharps or remove two flats ([saxstation](https://saxstation.com/tenor-sax-transposition-how-to.htm)), and the octave is then your choice. Concert-pitch melodies moved up a 9th sometimes climb into the palm keys. No source gives tenor-specific advice here, but the usual habit is to drop the whole phrase an octave rather than jump octaves in the middle of a phrase, and to match the register of the tenor recording you're copying.

## A free slow-downer covers Linux; buy one app at most

The tool that matters is a **slow-down-and-loop player** for the recording. On your Linux desktop, the free **"Transpose ▲▼" extension** for Chrome and Edge (about 900k users) gives YouTube speed control, unlimited A-B loops and semitone shifts ([Chrome Web Store](https://chrome.google.com/webstore/detail/transpose-pitch-loop-for/ioimlbgefgadofblnajllknopjboejda)). On Firefox, **Etude · Looper** saves named A-B loops per video ([Firefox Add-ons](https://addons.mozilla.org/firefox/addon/etude-looper/)). Even YouTube on its own works: the back arrow rewinds 10 seconds and the gear menu sets 75% with the pitch kept ([BetterSax](https://bettersax.com/how-to-learn-tunes-without-the-real-book/)). If you want saved per-phrase markers on audio files rather than YouTube, **Transcribe!** is the standard desktop tool and the only paid one with a native Linux build. It costs about **€37 one-time**, and it's on Flathub too. The Linux build lags the current release slightly (9.51.1 vs 9.60) ([Seventh String](https://seventhstring.com/xscribe/buy.html); [Flathub](https://flathub.org/hi/apps/com.seventhstring.Transcribe)). On a Mac or phone, **Anytune** gets the most sax-teacher endorsements, BetterSax's among them, and Pro+ is **$14.99** with no subscription ([BetterSax](https://bettersax.com/7-amazing-apps-for-saxophone-players-you-probably-didnt-know-existed/); [App Pricing Lab](https://www.apppricinglab.com/app/apple/478293637)). These tools overlap almost completely, so pick one per device. You don't need pitch-shifting to play along: a concert recording already sounds right under your written-Bb fingers. The fine-tune control is useful only for old records that run slightly off A=440.

Stem separation is a bonus, not a requirement. Your Demucs setup can already do what the paid "isolate" features in Anytune Studio, Transcribe+ and Moises charge for ([App Store, Anytune](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/anytune/id415365180); [App Store, Transcribe+](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1048119179)). Use it to remove a singer from a straight vocal version so you can play the melody over the original band (Demucs' `vocals` stem does detect real voices). Your own audio doc records that the same stem misses saxophone, so removing a *horn* head needs your `remove_sax.py` route. Moises advertises dedicated "Wind" and "Woodwind" stems ([Moises](https://moises.ai/features/stem-separation/)), but no independent test of them on a jazz-combo horn was found, so it isn't worth a subscription on spec.

The chart apps split cleanly into those that carry the melody and those that don't. **iReal Pro has no melody at all.** Its forum confirms "there is no facility inside iReal Pro to add melody," and LJS adds that its MIDI swing is "mechanical" ([iReal forums](https://forums.irealpro.com/goto/post?id=72534); [LJS](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/?p=184687)). It belongs in the later step of the method, reading the chart over a backing to prove you can keep your place, and not in the melody-learning step. PDF readers such as forScore and MobileSheets can't transpose a scanned fake book ([forScore support](https://forscore.freshdesk.com/support/solutions/articles/151000204975-transposing-and-music-playback)). That doesn't matter for you, because your Bb single-tune sheets already exist. For a tune not yet converted, MuseScore (free, Linux) makes the PDF and can also play the written melody back as a check. Treat MuseScore *community* uploads as a cross-check only: users report charts that "play back perfectly" but are notated wrongly, and lead sheets that play a whole step off ([MuseScore forum](https://musescore.org/en/node/322258); [MuseScore forum](https://musescore.org/en/node/289122)).

## Trust Sher for accuracy, the 557 for the band

The fake books you own rank clearly for melody accuracy. **Sher Music's books are the most accurate** in the opinion of a long-running fake-book guide, though their full arrangements can be crowded to read. The Hal Leonard 6th edition "corrected all the mistakes in the Fifth Edition" while introducing some new ones, and it is the book that guide recommends reading from on the gig ([Keller, The Joy of Fakebooks](https://www.cs.hmc.edu/~keller/jazz/TheJoyOfFakebooks.htm)). It is also what "everyone's tablet" has at a jam ([jazzguitar.be](https://www.jazzguitar.be/forum/songs/98423-hal-leonard-real-books-accuracy.html)). Some 6th-edition charts follow different recordings from the old bootleg, so the two editions don't always agree ([Wikipedia: Real Book](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Real_Book)). An independent errata list exists for the 6th edition ([DeCaro errata](https://james-decaro.squarespace.com/blog/2015/5/4/real-book-sixth-edition-errata)). That suggests a simple order for you. When a 557 note looks doubtful, check it first against your **Sher Standards Real Book (C)**, then against the **Hal Leonard 6th ed. Bb**, and use the old bootleg Vol. 2 last. The straight recording decides between them, but the 557 remains what you play at band. One caution: no publisher, review or legitimate Bb edition of the "557 Jazz Standards" turned up anywhere. The only "557 in Bb" pages online look like spam or piracy ([jazzguitar.be 557 thread](https://www.jazzguitar.be/forum/comping-chords-chord-progressions/2176-557-jazz-standards-pdf-3.html)). Check any Bb copy you receive against the C edition page by page before trusting it at band.

For hearing the *written* melody played, as opposed to a performer's version, one book series stands out. Each **Hal Leonard Jazz Play-Along** tune has a split track with the melody cue in one channel and the rhythm section in the other, plus a full no-melody track. Every volume carries C, Bb, Eb and bass-clef lead sheets, and the PLAYBACK+ player slows, loops and pans. Volumes cost about **$15–33**, across roughly 105 volumes ([Hal Leonard](https://www.halleonard.com/jazzplayalong)). Panning lets you hear the head alone, then mute it and play it yourself over the same band. That is the "listen, then play the melody" loop in one product. **Real Book Multi-Tracks** (15 volumes, $18–20, 10 songs each) have sax or trumpet playing the head, with mutable parts and a follow-along marker on the lead sheet ([Hal Leonard](https://www.halleonard.com/series/RBPLYA)). Aebersold volumes are rhythm section only: "YOU provide the melody" ([jazzbooks.com archive](https://web-wp.archive.org/web/20170125051607/http://www.jazzbooks.com:80/jazz/abpl)). Like your Playbook tracks, they suit the reading step and not the ear step. Tenor-specific **Instrumental Play-Along** books (12–14 tunes, about $17) include demo tracks ([Long & McQuade](https://www.long-mcquade.com/238826/Print-Music/Woodwind/Hal-Leonard/Jazz-Classics-Instrumental-Play-Along-Tenor-Sax-Book-Audio-Online.htm)). They are solo arrangements, though, and a buyer warns that some tenor "standards" folios aren't in the standard keys ([Musicnotes](https://www.musicnotes.com/sheet-music/scoring/instr-solo/tenor-saxophone/style/standards)). Check keys against the 557 before using one for band prep. **Tomplay** offers tenor jazz scores with a sax part you can mute and a scrolling score, Autumn Leaves among them, with a 14-day free trial ([Tomplay](https://tomplay.com/saxophone-sheet-music/real-book-jazz-by-tomplay-tenor-saxophone-advanced-vol-1-saxophone-tenor-saxophone-score); [BetterSax](https://bettersax.com/7-amazing-apps-for-saxophone-players-you-probably-didnt-know-existed/)). The trial is a cheap way to test that format on one band tune.

What's still unknown: whether every Jazz Play-Along volume uses sax for the melody cue (it varies by volume), whether the Quartet app's real-player tracks include a horn head, and whether any YouTube channel teaches standard heads by call and response. No such series could be confirmed.

## Conclusion

The resource question turns out to be smaller than it looks. The melody already exists in three places you have: the recording, the 557 page, and your own voice. The tools only make those three easier to compare. That changes what's worth buying. Almost nothing on the market *plays* the written head, apart from Hal Leonard's split-track books, and almost everything else (iReal Pro, Aebersold, your Playbook) is a backing band. So the gap in your kit isn't charts or backings, which you have plenty of. It's a melody-on/melody-off recording of a band tune, and a convenient looper.

The less obvious takeaway concerns your band's goal. Because you read at band, the ear work is there to make your reading *musical*, not to replace it. The step most methods skip, reading the chart along with the recording, is the one that turns "I know how it goes" into "I can follow the page and stay in my place." It also catches every spot where the 557 differs from what you heard, before rehearsal rather than during it.

## Recommended setup for you

**Toolkit**
- **Recordings:** YouTube or free Spotify, plus a library-card service (Naxos Music Library Jazz or Freegal) if your library offers one. Find versions through the jazzstandards.com "Getting Started" picks.
- **Slow-down and loop:** the free "Transpose" extension (Chrome/Edge) or Etude · Looper (Firefox) on Linux. Plain YouTube at 75% also works.
- **Charts:** your Bb single-tune sheets from the 557 for band. Check doubtful notes against the Sher Standards Real Book, then the Hal Leonard 6th ed. Bb.
- **Stems:** your Demucs setup, to remove the singer from a vocal version (use `remove_sax.py` for horn heads).
- **Backing for the reading step:** Playbook / Let's Play Jazz! tracks, Aebersold, or iReal Pro.
- **Worth paying for (at most these):**
  1. **One Hal Leonard Jazz Play-Along volume** (about $15–33) that contains band tunes. It's the only owned-format way to hear the head on its own and then mute it.
  2. **Transcribe!** (about €37, Linux), *or* **Anytune Pro+** ($14.99, Mac/iPhone) if you'd rather practise from the phone. Buy one, and only if the free extension starts to feel limiting.
  3. Optional: a **Tomplay** 14-day trial on one band tune before spending anything on it.

**Learning one standard's melody**
1. Pick two versions: a straight singer (Songbook tune) or the composer's recording (jazz original), plus one simple tenor version.
2. Listen with the horn put away until you can hum it. Learn the first lyric lines if there are any.
3. Sing the melody along with the straight version, then without it.
4. On tenor, take one short phrase at a time. Sing it, find it on the horn, then confirm it along with the recording looped at 75%. Choose one comfortable octave per phrase.
5. Open your Bb 557 sheet and play the head from the page along with the recording. Mark any note where the page and the record disagree, and play the page's version at band.
6. Read the head from the page over a backing track (Playbook, Aebersold, iReal Pro or a Jazz Play-Along no-melody track), keeping your place through the form.
7. Listen to the tenor version once more and copy one phrasing detail into your reading.
