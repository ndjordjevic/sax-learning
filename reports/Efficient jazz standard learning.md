# Hear it, map it, then play it

The most efficient way to learn a jazz standard is the one nearly every major educator converges on: **listen until you can sing the melody, learn the melody by ear and check it against the chart, learn the form and changes as a small number of named chunks, test yourself from memory early, and only then improvise, starting from roots and guide tones and keeping the melody as your frame**. Music-cognition research backs the two load-bearing parts of that recipe. Playing by ear is the "hub" skill that predicts improvising and playing from memory, and expert memorizers, including a jazz pianist studied learning a bebop head, remember a tune by its **formal structure and section landmarks**, not by a fragile bar-to-bar chain. For you, the weekly band is the most valuable tool you have. Hal Galper says a tune isn't really learned until it has been played at least three times in performance, and the band gives you that repetition every week. Your order of Autumn Leaves, then Blue Bossa, then All of Me is a sound one. All three appear on beginner lists and in real jam-call data, and each adds one new building block. A realistic pace is **one new tune every few weeks**, with the band's list doing most of the review. Your owned books fit specific steps rather than competing with each other: the Playbook covers the map and the guide tones, Let's Play Jazz! the chord drills for Autumn Leaves, Birch your first improvising, and Aebersold's Jazz Ear Training hearing the changes.

## Seven methods agree on one skeleton: listen, sing, then play

Four fully documented methods, Brent Vaartstra's LIST, Jamey Aebersold's 13 "Tips for Learning a New Tune", Hal Galper's "How To Learn A Tune" and Jazzadvice's three steps, share a core. They have you **listen many times, sing the melody, learn melody and changes (ideally by ear), check against paper, and improvise from memory**. Vaartstra's LIST (Listen, Internalize, Sing, Transfer) tells you not to touch the horn until you can sing the melody both with the recording and alone, and claims singing "takes away 50% of the learning process." Its advanced option is singing the bass roots to hear the harmony ([LJS, *Learn Jazz Standards the Smart Way*](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Learn-Jazz-Standards-the-Smart-Way.pdf)). This is the same LIST method printed on pp.15–19 of your Playbook. Aebersold's list is the most complete free checklist written for horn players. Its steps are: listen over and over, memorize and sing the melody, listen to the bass line, play the melody from memory slowly, then **play along with the recording copying "inflections, articulations, slurs, phrasing, dynamics"**, learn the chords in order, improvise "keeping in mind the original melody as a frame of reference," emphasize **3rds and 7ths**, and learn the lyrics ([Aebersold *Jazz Handbook*](https://www.jazzbooks.com/mm5/download/FQBK-handbook.pdf)). Bob Reynolds, Michelle Mailhot and the Open Studio podcast all put listening first as well ([Berklee Today](https://www.berklee.edu/node/2339871); [Clearwater Jazz PDF](https://www.clearwaterjazz.com/_uploaded_files/6_steps_to_learning_a_new_tune.pdf); [Open Studio](https://youllhearit.transistor.fm/episodes/how-to-learn-new-tunes)).

The research supports the ear-first principle. In McPherson's studies of 101 school instrumentalists, **playing by ear was the only one of five skills that correlated with all the others**, and path analysis showed it directly influencing improvising, sight-reading and playing from memory ([Corcoran & Spiro 2021](https://researchonline.rcm.ac.uk/id/eprint/1919/1/Corcoran%20%26%20Spiro%202021.pdf); [MIC thesis summary](https://dspace.mic.ul.ie/bitstreams/950fad9a-8780-429c-ac3a-7feeceff73c4/download)). Players who depend on the score are worse at reproducing *pitch* (though not rhythm) by ear ([Corcoran & Spiro 2021](https://researchonline.rcm.ac.uk/id/eprint/1919/1/Corcoran%20%26%20Spiro%202021.pdf)). The data are correlational and come from young players, so take this as the best bet available rather than proof. Singing also needs perspective. In a controlled trial with 50 trumpet students, **singing alone or imagining alone did no better than no practice**, but **singing, playing and imagining combined** was the only strategy that improved musical expression ([Steenstrup et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.757052/full)). Singing works as an extra layer on top of playing. It doesn't replace playing.

The methods disagree on three points. Each one resolves cleanly for your situation:

| Disagreement | Camps | Resolution for you |
|---|---|---|
| Melody first or structure first | Vaartstra and Aebersold start with the melody. Galper learns key, meter, length, form and harmonic landmarks first and the melody last ([halgalper.com](https://www.halgalper.com/?p=647)). | Do both early. Learn the melody by ear, but say the form and section landmarks out loud before your first band run so you can't get lost. |
| Paper | Open Studio says "stay away from the pages." Galper allows the chart for two passes, then "throw it away." Aebersold wants the changes "from a reliable source." | Use the 557 as the check and as the authority on the changes, because those are the chords your band plays. Your notes already record that the 557 harmony differs from the Playbook's on Autumn Leaves. |
| Play-alongs | Aebersold is built on them. Reynolds says to put them away and play roots against a metronome at about 80 bpm ([Berklee](https://www.berklee.edu/node/2339871)). | Use a metronome for roots and guide tones. Use backing tracks (Playbook, Let's Play Jazz!, iReal Pro) once you're improvising. |

Which recordings you use matters. Vaartstra's tip is "Frank Sinatra is your friend" because he sings melodies straight, and he suggests the composer's or original artist's version for less classic tunes ([LJS PDF](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Learn-Jazz-Standards-the-Smart-Way.pdf)). A university audition list makes the same point about Ella Fitzgerald and Sinatra ([University of Utah](https://music.utah.edu/documents/ensemble-audition-excerpts/jazz/2022/university_of_utah_jazz_tune_list_for_auditions.pdf)). For All of Me, that means a straight vocal version followed by a horn version. Blue Bossa is an instrumental by Kenny Dorham, so learn it from a horn recording such as Joe Henderson's *Page One*. Lyrics are worth the small effort on vocal tunes. Lester Young told players to know the words of any ballad they played, and **Dexter Gordon recited a ballad's opening lyric before counting it off** ([Wikipedia – Dexter Gordon](https://www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Dexter_Gordon)). Studies of singers find that words and melody are stored together ([Bulletproof Musician: Ginsborg](https://bulletproofmusician.com/jane-ginsborg-on-building-stronger-and-more-reliable-musical-memory/)). Whether this helps an instrumentalist's recall hasn't been tested.

## Memorize the road map, not just the fingers

Memory research has the most to offer on not getting lost in the form. Chaffin and Imreh tracked a concert pianist learning a Bach Presto and found she **"used the formal structure as a retrieval scheme and performance cues as retrieval cues."** Two years later, her recall was best at section starts and at the bars where she had placed cues ([Chaffin & Imreh 2002](https://musiclab.uconn.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/290/2013/10/Practicing-Perfection-Chaffin-2002.pdf)). The same pattern turned up in jazz. An experienced jazz pianist learning Hank Mobley's AABA *Funk in Deep Freeze* **started practice segments at subsection beginnings 12.5 times more often than elsewhere**, and analysed the chord progressions as an explicit memorization step ([Noice et al. 2008](https://production.wordpress.uconn.edu/musiclab/wp-content/uploads/sites/290/2013/10/MemoryJazzMusician.pdf)). Jane Ginsborg explains why this matters. Rote repetition builds *serial cueing*, where each bar triggers the next. That memory is quick to build but "easily disrupted," and **lapses cluster at section boundaries**. Landmark-based memory lets you jump back in at the next section ([Bulletproof Musician: Ginsborg](https://bulletproofmusician.com/jane-ginsborg-on-building-stronger-and-more-reliable-musical-memory/)). Danger spots are the "switches," places where two similar passages diverge, like the different endings of A1 and A2. Your approach to the switch gives no clue which way to go, so you have to know the structure.

For a standard, the map is the form plus the harmonic plan, learned as chunks rather than as 32 separate chords. Norgaard's corpus study found that **82.6% of the notes in 48 Charlie Parker solos begin a recurring four-interval pattern** ([Norgaard 2014](https://core.ac.uk/download/71427376.pdf)). In a second study, pianists given a distracting counting task while improvising leaned on stored patterns even more ([Norgaard et al. 2016](https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/music_facpub/79)). Pre-learned chunks free attention, and at a band rehearsal that is the attention you need for listening. Educators say the same thing in plainer terms: most standards are built from major and minor ii–V–Is and turnarounds, so learn the language of each block once and reuse it ([Jazzadvice – Blueprint](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/a-blueprint-for-building-your-repertoire/)). In practice, describe Autumn Leaves as "ii–V–I to the relative major, ii–V–i to the minor, AABC" rather than as eight chord symbols. Then practise starting from the top of each section, not only from bar 1.

Retrieval practice is how experts lock the map in. Both case studies show "memory runs," where the player performs without the score and treats every hesitation as a list of what to fix next ([Chaffin & Imreh 2002](https://musiclab.uconn.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/290/2013/10/Practicing-Perfection-Chaffin-2002.pdf)). Galper's version: read from paper the first two times, put it away the third time, and note what you couldn't remember, because "When you're looking you're not hearing!" He also runs through the tune mentally in bed and fixes the gaps the next day ([halgalper.com](https://www.halgalper.com/?p=647)). Sleep research supports doing new material late in a session. A new keyboard melody improved after a night's sleep but not after an equal waking interval ([UT Austin CML](https://cml.music.utexas.edu/research/skill-learning-and-procedural-memory)). Learning a *second* new melody straight afterwards wiped out the overnight gain, though briefly replaying the first melody offset that ([TDL thesis](https://tdl-ir.tdl.org/items/63f63d23-337b-441c-abae-ba630b03d571/full)). The broader spacing and interleaving evidence in music is weaker. One piano study found no spacing benefit ([Wiseheart et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5553926)), and interleaving tunes in a session helped in small studies without reliably reaching significance ([Carter & Grahn 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4989027)). So short, repeated contact with a tune over several days is a cheap default, not a proven optimum.

Expect a head to take several short sessions. The expert jazz pianist secured a short bebop head in **two sessions of 15 and 30 minutes, ten days apart** ([Noice et al. 2008](https://production.wordpress.uconn.edu/musiclab/wp-content/uploads/sites/290/2013/10/MemoryJazzMusician.pdf)). Amateurs on a guitar forum report about a week to get a tune "passable for a jam" ([Jazz Guitar Online](https://www.jazzguitar.be/forum/getting-started/81792-strategy-learning-jazz-standards.html)). For an advanced beginner, the head will probably come in a week or two of short contact, and the changes will take longer. That is an extrapolation, not a measured figure.

## Improvise up a ladder that starts at the roots

The improvisation sources agree on a ladder: **roots in time, then chord tones, then 3rds and 7ths (guide tones), then scales, then free playing with the melody as the frame**. Reynolds has you play only the roots in time for three choruses to learn the form, then "Think it. Say it. Play it.": say the roots, then the 3rds, triads and 7ths aloud in time. Wherever you hesitate is what to practise ([Berklee](https://www.berklee.edu/node/2339871)). Aebersold's 12-step drill walks through every chord of the tune in order: root, scale fragments, triad, 7th chord, 9th, full scale. He says that once these are in your vocabulary, you won't need the drill for each new tune ([Aebersold *Jazz Handbook*](https://www.jazzbooks.com/mm5/download/FQBK-handbook.pdf)). His improvising advice is to emphasize 3rds and 7ths, begin and end phrases on chord tones, and lift ideas from the original recording ([same](https://www.jazzbooks.com/mm5/download/FQBK-handbook.pdf)). Pressing's model of improvisation calls the tune itself the **"referent,"** the guiding scheme that generates and edits what you play ([Frontiers 2017, citing Pressing](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00966/pdf)). That is why improvising off the melody comes early, and why the melody has to be secure first.

Your books already cover each rung, so this is a matter of order rather than buying anything. The Playbook's first improvisation lesson is guide tones, and its later studies move on to the melody and chord tones. Let's Play Jazz! Ch.3 covers root–5th bass lines, arpeggios and scales over the same Autumn Leaves changes, though it gets steep from there. Birch is the pentatonic on-ramp: A minor pentatonic fits every Autumn Leaves chord on tenor without yet "making the changes." It builds phrasing and confidence while the guide tones are still slow. Aebersold's Jazz Ear Training handles the hearing side. Ex.19 trains walking bass and Ex.25 ii–V7–I phrases, which matches Vaartstra's advice to identify the bass note and then the chord quality ([LJS PDF](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Learn-Jazz-Standards-the-Smart-Way.pdf)). One gap in the sources: none of the retrieved methods makes "learn licks" a tune-learning step. The closest is Aebersold's "incorporate ideas off the recording." Norgaard's pattern findings suggest a small personal bank of one or two lines per ii–V, taken from the tune's own recordings, is still worth building. His later work also finds that each improviser's bank is idiosyncratic, so borrowed lines are a starting point and not the goal ([Norgaard et al. 2023](https://clarivate.forskningsportal.dk/display/pub-wos-000885281100002)).

## Grow the repertoire by families and let the band do the spacing

Pick tunes by usefulness, not by global rankings. A London musician logged **2,038 calls of 308 songs**: the top 25 covered only **36%** of calls, but All of Me, Autumn Leaves and Blue Bossa were all among them ([StandardRepertoire](https://standardrepertoire.com/pages/the-top-25-jazz-standards.html)). LJS's "20 Easy Jazz Standards" explains why your three are good starters. Autumn Leaves is a circle of fourths with relative major and minor ii–V–Is. Blue Bossa is a 16-bar form with major and minor ii–V–Is and one key change ([LJS](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/20-easy-jazz-standards/)). All of Me then adds a 32-bar major-key tune with secondary dominants. Since the top 25 covers only a third of calls even in a big scene, **your band's own 57-tune list is a better guide than any published one**. Within it, take the Playbook tunes first, then group the rest into families so each new tune reuses learned chunks: blues heads (one 12-bar form, many melodies, Watermelon Man already on the list), 16-bar minor and Latin tunes, AABA ii–V tunes such as There Will Never Be Another You, and rhythm changes last. Contrafacts are the extreme case: dozens of bebop heads sit on the same rhythm-changes chords ([Wikipedia – Rhythm changes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhythm_changes)).

On pace, the sources favour depth. The LJS podcast's "One Jazz Standard Strategy" gives a whole month to one tune ([LJS podcast](https://metacast.app/podcast/learn-jazz-standards-podcast/D6LstnmH/the-one-jazz-standard-strategy-for-jazz/PLIzqXRE)). Its founder learned 100 standards in a year but wouldn't advise students to go that fast ([LJS podcast](https://metacast.app/podcast/learn-jazz-standards-podcast/D6LstnmH/lessons-from-learning-100-jazz-standards-in-a-year/6tQnuhFP)). Jazzadvice warns against cramming and against working on ten tunes at once ([Jazzadvice – Blueprint](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/a-blueprint-for-building-your-repertoire/)). Amateurs typically carry **10–20 tunes** ([Jazz Guitar Online](https://www.jazzguitar.be/forum/getting-started/81792-strategy-learning-jazz-standards.html)). One new tune every few weeks, each taken to "head from memory and follow the form," reaches that range within a year. Set a modest standard for "done": melody from memory and the form in your head. Improvising on the tune keeps deepening for years, and Jazzadvice says you are never really finished with a tune.

Maintenance is simpler than learning. The retention advice every source gives is to **play the tune regularly, ideally with other people** ([LJS](https://learnjazzstandards.com/blog/how-to-learn-a-jazz-standard-and-not-forget-it)). The author of the Jazztunes tracker found that at about 100 tunes he "had difficulty keeping them all straight," and built a tool that records when each tune was last played ([Jazztunes](https://jazztunes.readthedocs.io/en/dev/about/whyjazztunes.html)). For by-ear tunes, an Irish-fiddle teacher suggests replaying a new tune at the end of the session, then the next day, then two days later, at loosening intervals ([Hannah Harris](https://hannah-harris-ceol.kit.com/posts/a-scientific-way-to-remember-more-tunes)). A pianists' forum adds that review can be limited to melody and changes, the "framework" ([PianoGroove](https://community.pianogroove.com/t/keeping-up-your-repertoire/8577)). You don't need a timetable for this. Your weekly band already provides spaced repetition for whatever it plays, and the only extra habit is to play the "solid" tune you've gone longest without. No jazz-specific study has tested review intervals, so any interval rule is a heuristic.

Your tools fit around this. iReal Pro offers loops of any span, a global Bb transposition, automatic tempo increase on each repeat, and key change on each repeat ([iReal Pro FAQ](https://technimo.helpshift.com/hc/en/3-ireal-pro/faq/326-practicing-tips)). The last of these is a quick check on whether you know a tune by ear and function or only by fingering. LJS's warning is that it becomes a crutch when you read the screen instead of listening ([LJS – iReal Pro](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/?p=184687)). A slow-downer or your Demucs pipeline can isolate the bass of a favourite recording so you can hear the roots. On the horn side, tenor reads a whole step above concert, and the chord symbols move too ([Jazzfuel](https://jazzfuel.com/saxophone-transposition-guide/)). Keep making the single-tune Bb lead sheets from the 557, but talk keys with the band in concert.

## Conclusion

Learning a tune and remembering it turn out to be the same work. The things that make a tune memorable (hearing it, singing it, naming its sections and chunks, recalling it without the page) are also what improvising is built from. Pressing's "referent" and Chaffin's "retrieval scheme" are the same map seen from two sides. So for an advanced beginner the most efficient step isn't more scales or licks per tune. It is getting each tune's map clear enough to keep your place in the form, and that also makes the next tune cheaper because its chunks repeat. The main open question is evidence specific to amateurs. Every timing and pacing figure here is extrapolated from experts or forum anecdote, so adjust to what your own memory runs show.

Your situation also changes the usual advice. Most methods assume jam sessions where nobody reads. In a band that reads from the 557, the chart is unavoidable, so it's best treated as the authority on the changes and as Galper's "first two passes" before you put it away. That means the weekly rehearsal does two jobs: it is the performance context Galper says memorization needs, and it is the spaced review the research recommends.

## Recommended method

**For each new tune**
1. Make a short playlist: one straight version (a Sinatra or Ella vocal if the tune has words, the original horn version if not) and two or three others. Listen until you can hum the melody.
2. Sing the melody with the recording, then without it. Learn the first lyric lines if there are any.
3. Find the melody on the horn by ear. Check it against your Bb sheet from the 557 and fix it there, because that is the band's version.
4. Name the map out loud: key, form (AABA, ABAC, 16-bar, blues), the ii–V–Is and turnarounds, and where similar sections differ.
5. Sing the bass roots along with the recording. Then play the roots in time, then the 3rds and 7ths, slowly with a metronome.
6. Play the melody from memory, starting from the top of each section, not only bar 1. Wherever you hesitate is what to work on next.
7. At band, read the chart the first two times, then try the head without it and look only where you get stuck.
8. Improvise: embellish the melody first, then guide tones and chord tones (Playbook lessons), and add pentatonic phrases (Birch) or one borrowed line per ii–V. Use the backing tracks here.
9. Call it done when you can play the head from memory and always know where you are in the form. Then start the next tune.

**Keeping the repertoire**
1. Keep one list of band tunes marked learning / head from memory / solid.
2. In each session, play through from memory the solid tune you've gone longest without (melody and roots), then go back to the new tune.
3. Pick the next tune from the band list, Playbook tunes first (Blue Bossa, then All of Me), then tunes from the same family.
4. Keep doing the Aebersold ear-training exercises alongside, so hearing the changes gets easier with each tune.
