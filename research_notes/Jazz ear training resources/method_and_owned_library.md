## Q1. In what order should a beginner learn skills to hear chord changes?

### Takeaway
The educators agree on a bottom-up order:
1. intervals
2. chord qualities (maj7 / m7 / dom7 / m7b5)
3. **root/bass movement**, which every source calls the most direct route
4. hearing ii-V(-I) as one sound, with the key centre / Roman-numeral function
5. form and signposts (turnarounds, bridge), then whole standards from recordings

Singing is the thread that runs through every stage.

### Cited Findings
- Learn Jazz Standards (LJS) lays out four steps. (1) Understand chord progressions: the diatonic 7th chords and ii-V-I. (2) Recognise intervals, 13 ascending plus 13 descending: "Identifying intervals is the first step you need to take to identify chords by ear." (3) Recognise chord qualities: major, minor, dominant 7, half-diminished. (4) Recognise progressions: find the roots from the bass intervals, then the quality of each chord, then the key centre. — [LJS, "Ear Training Chord Progressions: How To Identify Chords By Ear In 4 Steps"](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/learning-jazz/ear-training/ear-training-chord-progressions/)
- LJS podcast ep. 80 gives the same order: Roman-numeral thinking, then bass notes, then each chord relative to I or to the previous chord, then quality as a hint. It presents progressions as "an important next step" after intervals and chords. — [LJS 80](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/ljs-podcast/jazz-tips-and-advice/ljs-80-chord-progressions-identify-ear/)
- LJS podcast ep. 174 suggests another entry point. Find the cadences and resolution points first, then the roots at those points, then the chords in between. It says "technically, you don't even need your instrument". It also admits that progressions are "so much harder than simply learning a melody." — [LJS, "How to Learn Chord Progressions By Ear (Step-by-Step)"](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/?p=133649)
- Jazzadvice:
  - Calls hearing progressions the next step after intervals and melodies.
  - Says "the most direct way to figure out a chord progression is to focus on the roots of all the chords", meaning the bass.
  - After the roots, identify chord quality and listen for ii-Vs, especially before resolutions and new key centres.
  - When stuck, take clues from the soloist's arpeggiated lines.
  
  — [Jazzadvice, "How to Hear Chord Changes"](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/how-to-hear-chord-changes/)
- On form, Jazzadvice says: "Think BIG FIRST, then small." Map the sections and bar counts by ear (e.g. AABA), mark the transitions, and listen for signposts: the bridge, a move to the relative minor, a brief modulation, a feel change, unusual root motion. "If you can't effortlessly hear the form, you don't actually know it." — [Jazzadvice, "Jazz Form and Structure: Never Get Lost in the Chord Changes Again"](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/never-get-lost-in-chord-changes-hear-form-anticipate-chords/)
- Jazzadvice's root progression for singing: (1) the sound of the root on a single V7; (2) a 12-bar blues backing track, singing roots; (3) a jazz blues; (4) the original record with full band (Miles Davis, *Trane's Blues*, *Workin'*); (5) eventually every chord tone of maj, min, V7 and half-diminished chords. — [Jazzadvice, "Can Your Ears Pass This Simple Test?"](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/ear-training-test/)
- Jerry Coker, Bob Knapp and Larry Vincent's *Hearin' the Changes* (Advance Music) is subtitled "Dealing with unknown tunes by ear". It catalogs the chord progressions of hundreds of standards by progression type, and its Appendix B has ear-training tips. (Details are from retailer listings; I could not open the book.) — [Alfred listing](https://www.alfred.com/products/hearin-the-changes-01-adv14270); [hebu-music listing](https://www.hebu-music.com/en/article/jerry-coker/advance-music-veronika-gruber-gmbh/hearin-the-changes-in-englischer-sprache.290369)
- Jamey Aebersold's *Jazz Ear Training* is described as starting "very simply, with intervals" and building until "you are hearing chord changes and progressions". This is a secondary listing description. — [search-result listing via Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/1038355)

### Inferences
- Every source puts the bass/roots first after basic intervals and qualities. For this reader that means: sing the roots of *Autumn Leaves* along with the Playbook backing track. He already has the chart and can follow the form, so this is the natural first exercise.
- The ii-V "chunk" is the second big milestone. *Autumn Leaves* is entirely major and minor ii-V-Is, so it can serve as both the training ground and the test piece.

## Q2. What specific practical techniques do teachers recommend?

### Takeaway
The core toolkit:
- sing the roots (bass line) with records and backing tracks
- play roots, then root-5th, two-feel bass lines on the horn along with the track
- play and sing guide tones (3rds and 7ths)
- play arpeggios through the changes
- learn tunes from recordings using a listen → internalize → sing → transfer process
- check what you hear against a chart only after attempting it by ear

### Cited Findings
- **Singing roots and bass lines:**
  - Jazzadvice: play a chord and sing its root. Its "test" has you sing every root on a real record, without knowing the key and without an instrument. — [Jazzadvice, How to Hear Chord Changes](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/how-to-hear-chord-changes/); [Jazzadvice, Ear Training Test](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/ear-training-test/)
  - LJS ep. 79 drill: hear a chord, then sing its root, 3rd, 5th or 7th. — [LJS 79](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/ljs-podcast/jazz-tips-and-advice/ljs-79-power-training-ear-hear-chords/) (search-snippet level only; page not opened)
- **With a walking bass, listen on beats 1 and 3**, "these are where the root notes will be played". Sing the melody first, then find the bass line and check the chord quality on a piano. — [Jazzadvice, "How to Learn Jazz Standards with the Piano"](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/how-to-learn-jazz-standards-with-the-piano/)
- **Learning tunes by ear: LJS's "LIST" process** (Listen, Internalize, Sing, Transfer). Sing the bass notes to learn the harmony. To transfer the chords, combine harmony knowledge with ear training to identify each chord's quality and function. Pro Tip #3: "Listen to the bass notes". If you get stuck, look at the sheet music while the recording plays, "starting and stopping … to see if you can decipher what's going on by ear." — Vaartstra, *The Jazz Standards Playbook Vol. 1*, pp. 15–19 (owned; see Q5). The same process appears in LJS podcast/blog material: [LJS 80](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/ljs-podcast/jazz-tips-and-advice/ljs-80-chord-progressions-identify-ear/)
- **Descending-interval trap in bass lines:** convert descending intervals to their ascending inversions (e.g. P4 down = P5 up). LJS gives a full table. — [LJS 80](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/ljs-podcast/jazz-tips-and-advice/ljs-80-chord-progressions-identify-ear/)
- **Guide tones (3rds and 7ths):**
  - The ii-V-I guide-tone line works because the 7th of one chord resolves a half step to the 3rd of the next. — [guitar-study-room (minor source)](https://guitar-study-room.pages.dev/en/courses/improv-guide-tones)
  - The Berklee-style "singing the changes" drill: sing roots in time over a held bass, then sing only 3rds and 7ths. The source is a marketing blog for an app (fifths-marketing.pages.dev), so treat it as low reliability. — [fifths-marketing, "Singing the changes"](https://fifths-marketing.pages.dev/blog/singing-the-changes-berklee/)
  - Owned books make the same point; see Q5 (Playbook p. 36–37; Standards Workbook p. 7).
- **Comparing and checking against recordings:**
  - Don't trust the Real Book alone. Compare several recordings, starting with a clear vocal version, and avoid reharmonised versions at first. Mulgrew Miller: "the answers are all there in the recording." — [Jazzadvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/how-to-hear-chord-changes/)
  - LJS suggests starting with simple pop songs that have a single guitar or piano accompaniment, e.g. I-V-vi-IV, and checking your answers against charts. — [LJS 4 Steps](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/learning-jazz/ear-training/ear-training-chord-progressions/)
- **Anticipating chords:** keep about 70/30 attention on the current versus the next chord and narrate the upcoming chord. Practise the first 8 bars without sheet music. — [Jazzadvice, Jazz Form](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/never-get-lost-in-chord-changes-hear-form-anticipate-chords/)
- **Functional ear training (scale degrees relative to a key):**
  - Bruce Arnold teaches "contextual ear training": hear each pitch in relation to the key. — [Backstage profile](https://www.backstage.com/magazine/article/ear-training-bruce-arnold-64217/)
  - The Functional Ear Trainer app sets the key with I-IV-V-I and then asks you to identify a tone. Free software by Alain Benbassat. — [Advancing Musician](https://advancingmusician.com/functional-ear-training); [Substack "The Music Mind"](https://jerkmakesmusic.substack.com/p/how-to-develop-your-sense-of-pitch)
  - Charlie Banacos is cited as a well-known jazz teacher of functional ear training. This is a forum claim. — [jazzguitar.be thread](https://www.jazzguitar.be/forum/ear-training-transcribing-reading/46372-journal-performance-ear-training-8.html)
- **Playing arpeggios over changes:** forum advice is to play 1-3-5-7 up and down over the changes many times. — [talkbass thread](https://www.talkbass.com/threads/ear-training-for-jazz-fusion.1298060/) (anecdotal). Owned methods prescribe the same thing; see Q5 (Let's Play Jazz! Ch. 3; Birch *Rhythm Changes* Ch. 2).

### Inferences
- A practical ladder for a horn player:
  1. Sing roots with the backing track.
  2. Play roots on tenor with the track.
  3. Play root-5th two-feel.
  4. Play and sing guide tones.
  5. Play arpeggios.
  6. Repeat on the original recording.
  7. Try a new tune from records before looking at the chart.

  The owned books already contain almost every rung of this ladder (see Q5).

## Q3. How long does it take, and what are the most common mistakes?

### Takeaway
No reputable source gives a timeline. They describe the skill as slow and frustrating at first, and say it improves only with repetition and exposure. The commonly named mistakes:
- memorising chord symbols instead of the sound
- listening only to the top line (a typical horn-player habit)
- starting with reharmonised or complex recordings
- misreading descending bass intervals
- leaning on the chart or the horn instead of the voice

### Cited Findings
- Jazzadvice:
  - Calls the skill "frustrating and time consuming at first."
  - "Hearing progressions is not like having perfect pitch, where you either have it or you don't."
  - "This is a skill that must be developed in the practice room."
  - "Your focus should always be on the sound of the chords, not on memorizing chord symbols."
  - Horn players' ears go to upper structures by habit, so hearing the bass takes deliberate effort.
  
  — [Jazzadvice, How to Hear Chord Changes](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/how-to-hear-chord-changes/)
- LJS: "When it comes down to it, it's all about exposure. You need to hear chord progressions over and over again." — [LJS 80](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/ljs-podcast/jazz-tips-and-advice/ljs-80-chord-progressions-identify-ear/)
- LJS on mistakes: a descending 4th in the bass "does not mean it's a I-IV progression". Don't assume the first chord is I. Don't start with complex material. — [LJS 4 Steps](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/learning-jazz/ear-training/ear-training-chord-progressions/)
- Jazzadvice: "You can't hide behind your instrument and music theory." — [Jazzadvice, Ear Training Test](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/ear-training-test/)
- One chord-trainer blog claims that "most people begin to track chord changes consistently within four to six weeks" at 10 minutes a day. This is unsupported marketing copy; don't rely on it. — [chordna.com](https://chordna.com/blog/how-to-train-your-ear-to-hear-chord-changes)
- A forum anecdote: the more tunes you know and play, the easier it gets to hear the common changes. — via [jazzguitar.be forum](https://www.jazzguitar.be/forum/ear-training-transcribing-reading/92760-how-hear-chords.html)
- Vaartstra warns against skipping the listening stage. Students rush into a tune "having barely listened to it". — *Jazz Standards Playbook Vol. 1*, p. 15 (owned)

### Inferences
- Short, frequent sessions tied to the tunes the band actually plays are the advice most consistent with "exposure". The reader's weekly band tunes are a natural supply of material.

### Gaps
- There is no credible data on time-to-competence. Treat any specific number as unsupported.

## Q4. What can a tenor player do without a piano? Is a keyboard or piano app recommended?

### Takeaway
Most work can be done with **voice + horn + recordings/backing tracks**. Root singing, bass-line playing and guide tones need no piano, and LJS says you "don't even need your instrument". Educators still widely recommend some basic keyboard work, such as two-note shells and checking chord qualities, because horns never sound chords. A cheap keyboard or a piano app is enough for this.

### Cited Findings
- Jazzadvice: playing two-note voicings of a standard at the piano "might be the most overlooked, yet most beneficial thing you can do to learn the harmony of a tune"; "if you can't hear it, you can't learn it." — [Jazzadvice (search snippet, p=11422)](https://www.jazzadvice.com/?p=11422); [Jazzadvice, How to Learn Jazz Standards with the Piano](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/how-to-learn-jazz-standards-with-the-piano/)
- Jazzadvice's article on piano basics for non-pianists says you don't need to be a gifted pianist to benefit. — [Jazzadvice, Piano Basics: Necessary Skills for the Non-Pianist](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/piano-basics-necessary-skills-for-the-non-pianist/) (snippet level)
- Jeremy Kahn: horns "don't play chords (in the way that guitars or pianos do)", so the sound of chords is unfamiliar to horn players. — [jeremykahn.co.uk](https://jeremykahn.co.uk/2014/04/02/play-it-by-ear-2/)
- A clinic handout, "Jazz piano for horn players", says playing chords aids understanding of tunes and memorisation. It recommends Levine's *The Jazz Piano Book* and Mintzer's *Playing Jazz Piano*. — [clearwaterjazz.com PDF](https://www.clearwaterjazz.com/_uploaded_files/jazz_piano_for_horn_players.pdf) (via search snippet)
- "Learn piano first", attributed to Dizzy Gillespie advising Charlie Parker, is unsourced forum lore. — [trumpetherald forum](https://www.trumpetherald.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=1697592)
- No-piano alternatives:
  - The Functional Ear Trainer app (free).
  - Drone apps such as MetroDrone for singing scale degrees against a tonic. — [Advancing Musician](https://advancingmusician.com/functional-ear-training); [Muse EEK MetroDrone](https://muse-eek.com/?p=1359)
- In the owned books, single-line instruments are told to **arpeggiate** guide-tone and root shapes: "On single-note instruments, you'll have to arpeggiate it like this." — *The Standards Workbook* (owned; see Q5)

### Inferences
- A cheap keyboard or a piano app (e.g. on a phone or tablet) is enough. Its only jobs are to sound a chord, play shells (root plus 3rd and 7th), and check guesses. No piano technique is needed.
- Everything else can be done on the tenor with the backing tracks the reader already owns.

### Gaps
- I found no source comparing a physical keyboard with an app for this purpose.

## Q5. What ear-training material does the owned library already contain? (Part B)

### Takeaway
The library has **no dedicated "hear the chord changes" course**. It does contain a strong set of chord-hearing exercises with audio, all in owned books with tenor or Bb audio:
- the Playbook's LIST process and guide-tone charts
- *Let's Play Jazz!*'s bass-line exercise on the *Autumn Leaves* changes
