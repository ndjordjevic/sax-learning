# Choosing recordings and learning a jazz standard's melody by ear (tenor sax, advanced beginner, band reads from the 557)

Research date: 2026-11-22. About 15 tool calls. The sources are mostly educator blogs, plus one free PDF guide, one university handout and reference sites. I found no Reddit or Sax on the Web threads that were worth citing.

## (a) How do respected educators recommend choosing the reference recordings (vocal vs composer/original vs sax), how many, and why lyrics matter?

### Takeaway
The educators agree on three points:
- **For the "true" melody**, use a singer who sings it straight (Sinatra is the standard example), the composer's recording, or the original show/film version.
- **For your first version**, use a simple, clear, often older recording.
- **For style and feel**, listen to several jazz versions, then fall in love with one of them. Learning the lyrics is a standard tip because it fixes the melody and phrasing in memory.

No source gives an exact number of versions. The ranges are "a couple" (the user's plan), "three or four" (one summary), and "as many as possible" (Vaartstra).

### Cited Findings
- **Go to the source.** Check the composer's own recording, or the non-jazz version from the Broadway show or film the tune came from. Example: the 1943 film clip of "My Shining Hour." — [Brent Vaartstra, "How To Learn The Melody Of A Jazz Standard By Ear," LJS, 2015-09-29](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/?p=23884)
- **"Know your jazz musicians."** Billie Holiday takes many liberties with melodies. "Frank Sinatra on the other hand almost always sings the melody straight." Avoid Miles Davis for melody accuracy unless the tune is his own. "Know who to listen to for feeling and who to listen to for accuracy." — [Vaartstra, LJS](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/?p=23884)
- **"Frank Sinatra is your friend."** For a less classic song, find the original composer or the artist who first performed it, because they will likely play the melody "true to its original intent." — [Vaartstra, *Learn Jazz Standards the Smart Way* (free PDF), Pro Tip #2](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Learn-Jazz-Standards-the-Smart-Way.pdf)
- **Split by tune type.** For Great American Songbook tunes, use singers such as Sinatra for straight melodies. For tunes written by jazz musicians (in this list: Doxy, Now's the Time, Blue Bossa, Perdido, Satin Doll), use the original recording by the composer or the original band. — [LJS Podcast, "How Do I Know the Real Melody to a Jazz Standard?", 2024-02-28](https://www.everand.com/podcast/709265483/How-Do-I-Know-the-Real-Melody-to-a-Jazz-Standard)
- **"Find as many recordings as possible of the jazz standard you want to learn and go through all of them,"** with the instrument put away. — [Vaartstra, Smart Way PDF, "Listen"](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Learn-Jazz-Standards-the-Smart-Way.pdf)
- **Jazzadvice (Forrest Wernick): pick a "simple and clear" recording first.** That means an audible bass line and comping, a clearly played melody, no reharmonization, and a tempo slow enough to follow. "In most cases it will be more advantageous to start out with a simpler, and often older, version." Then check other simple versions, and only later move to complex or modern ones. "One recording is not the stopping point." The article cites a vocal *Stella by Starlight* as a good model because the singer "sings the melody very precisely." — [Jazzadvice, "3 Tricks to Learning Tunes From Recordings…"](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/3-tricks-to-learning-tunes-from-recordings-that-have-nothing-to-do-with-practicing/)
- **Same article, Trick 3:** also find a version you love and "listen obsessively" (commute, workouts), paying attention to its vibe. It can be a different recording from the simple reference one. — [Jazzadvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/3-tricks-to-learning-tunes-from-recordings-that-have-nothing-to-do-with-practicing/)
- **Jazzadvice (Eric):** "find an easy and clear recording of the tune to learn from." For interpretation, "find different versions of the song to emulate, thinking about tempo, sound, feel." — [Jazzadvice, "How to Learn a New Jazz Tune Every Day"](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/how-to-learn-a-new-jazz-tune-every-day/)
- **Another Jazzadvice article** recommends comparing multiple recordings to find the "definitive" version you're likely to meet on the bandstand. Seen only as a search-result summary, not fetched. — [Jazzadvice (via search)](https://www.jazzadvice.com/?p=120107)
- **BetterSax (Jay Metcalf):** for beginners, "choose a recording you like at a slow or medium tempo and learn to play the tune just like it is played on the record." — [BetterSax, "How to Learn Tunes (Without the Real Book)"](https://bettersax.com/how-to-learn-tunes-without-the-real-book/)
- **Lyrics:** "Learning lyrics is extremely helpful for internalizing melodies… [standards] are poetic, memorable, and tell the story and essence of the song." — [Vaartstra, Smart Way PDF, Pro Tip #1](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Learn-Jazz-Standards-the-Smart-Way.pdf). The article adds that words make a melody easier to memorize and reveal its story, and that bebop heads usually have no lyrics, so use a slow-downer for those instead. — [Vaartstra, LJS](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/?p=23884)
- **Lester Young and lyrics.** The well-known story is that Young insisted on knowing the words. One sourced version: Frank Sinatra praised Young's knowledge of the lyrics to the songs he played (Sinatra interview with Arlene Francis, as quoted in Michael Robinson's essay). — [Azure Miles, "Returning to Lester Young"](https://azuremilesrecords.com/returningtolesteryoung.html). Forum posters add that Young stopped mid-solo because he forgot the words, that Dexter Gordon recited lyrics before ballads, and that Miles Davis stressed lyrics. These are anecdotal and unverified. — [jazzguitar.be forum thread on the importance of lyrics](https://www.jazzguitar.be/forum/songs/41676-importance-knowing-lyrics-jazz-standards.html)

### Inferences
- **A practical recipe** for each band tune, synthesized from the sources above:
  1. One straight vocal or original version, for the melody and lyrics.
  2. One simple, clear instrumental version, ideally tenor.
  3. Optionally, one "love it" version for feel.

  This matches the user's "a couple of versions" goal and covers both accuracy and style.
- **Songbook tunes** on the band list are All of Me, Autumn Leaves, Body and Soul, Bye Bye Blackbird, Georgia, Summertime, Stella by Starlight, and There Will Never Be Another You. A straight singer is the melody reference for these.
- **Jazz-composed tunes** are Doxy (Sonny Rollins), Now's the Time (Charlie Parker), Blue Bossa (Kenny Dorham, on Joe Henderson's *Page One*), Perdido (Juan Tizol, for Ellington), and Satin Doll (Ellington/Strayhorn). The original recording is the reference for these. These composer and album attributions are standard jazz knowledge; I did not verify them in this session.
- **Tenor reference versions** (Dexter Gordon, Sonny Rollins, Stan Getz, Coleman Hawkins, Ben Webster, Lester Young, Hank Mobley) are good for feel and sound. They also show that heads are routinely embellished, so check them against a straight vocal or original version. That last point is my inference from the "who to listen to for feeling vs accuracy" principle.

### Gaps
- **Missing educators:** I found nothing quotable from Hal Galper, Barry Harris students, Open Studio, Nathan Graybeal (Jazz Lesson Videos), or Michael League on choosing reference recordings. Searches did not reach their material.
- **Ella Fitzgerald and Chet Baker:** no source specifically rates them as "straight" melody sources. Ella's Songbook albums are commonly cited this way, but I did not verify that.
- **Lester Young quote:** I found no primary source (such as Lewis Porter's *A Lester Young Reader*) for the exact advice "know the lyrics."

## (b) Which resources help find recommended recordings and learn melodies by ear (sites, books, playlists, YouTube/call-and-response, slow-down apps, free legal listening)?

### Takeaway
- **Best per-tune recording lists:** jazzstandards.com has a "Getting Started" (student) pick and a CD list on every tune page. Ted Gioia's *The Jazz Standards* has about a dozen recommended versions per tune, and a roughly 2,000-track playlist built from it exists.
- **Slow-down tools:** YouTube's own speed control is enough to start. Anytune and Transcribe+ (both with AI stem isolation) are the leading apps.
- **Call-and-response videos:** I could not confirm any specific series.
- **Free legal listening:** YouTube, Spotify free, and library-card services (Naxos Music Library Jazz, Freegal, Hoopla).

## (c) What concrete step-by-step melody-by-ear process do teachers recommend, and how should ear and chart be combined when the goal is reading the 557 at band?

### Takeaway
The consensus process (Vaartstra's LIST: Listen → Internalize → Sing → Transfer) has five stages:
1. Listen a lot with the horn away.
2. Listen with full attention.
3. Learn the lyrics and sing the melody, first with the record and then without it.
4. Find the melody on the horn phrase by phrase (4-bar, then 8-bar chunks), slowing the record down if needed.
5. Check doubtful spots against the chart.

Educators say the sheet music is a checking tool. Others (Cipolla) keep the chart in front of them until the tune is second nature, which fits a goal of reading at band.

### Cited Findings
- **The LIST process:**
  - **Listen:** put the instrument away and go through many recordings.
  - **Internalize:** "intentional listening," with full attention and no distractions, like watching a film.
  - **Sing:** sing (or whistle or hum) the melody "both along with the recording and on your own before you learn it on your instrument." Singing "takes away 50% of the learning process."
  - **Transfer:** "pick up your instrument (you shouldn't have been touching it until now)," find the notes you can already sing, then work on the chords.

  — [Vaartstra, Smart Way PDF](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Learn-Jazz-Standards-the-Smart-Way.pdf)
- **Optional extra step:** sing the bass roots to start hearing the harmony. — [Smart Way PDF, Pro Tips #2–3](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Learn-Jazz-Standards-the-Smart-Way.pdf)
- **Sheet music's role:** it "can also be an excellent resource for checking the work your ear has done… a chord or two you are unsure about or a piece of the melody that seems ambiguous." When stuck, go through the chart *while listening to a recording*, starting and stopping. — [Smart Way PDF](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Learn-Jazz-Standards-the-Smart-Way.pdf)
- **Don't play along at the transfer step.** "You should not be listening to a recording while learning the melody on your instrument!" Once it's in your ear you can play it in any key. "If you can sing it you can play it." — [Vaartstra, LJS](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/?p=23884)
- **Contrasting view (BetterSax):** learn melodies "by playing along with recordings." Loop a section and play it "over and over again along with the recording until you can match what you're hearing," at 75% speed, or 50% if fast. Metcalf calls transcribing "the single most effective thing you can do to improve your musicianship." He also says reading sheet music is fine: "there's nothing wrong with that." — [BetterSax](https://bettersax.com/how-to-learn-tunes-without-the-real-book/)
- **BetterSax's "Sunny" lesson** (as summarized in search results):
  1. "listen to the recording several times to get the melody in your ear."
  2. Find the melody using the pentatonic scale.
  3. "Start with four-bar phrases, then eight-bar phrases. Don't try to learn the whole song all at once."
  4. Play each phrase with a metronome.

  — [BetterSax, melodies and fundamentals](https://bettersax.com/learn-melodies-by-ear-practice-saxophone/) (search summary; page not fetched)
- **Jazzadvice (Eric):**
  1. Listen a few times, then "try to sing the melody by yourself," repeating until confident.
  2. Break the melody into intervals or chord tones and sing each phrase until it's ingrained.
  3. Use the melody to hint at the harmony and the other way round.
  4. A piano is "an invaluable tool for figuring out each detail."
  5. Finally, interpret: phrasing, sound, feel ("tell a musical story").

  There is no fixed timeline, only one example "20 minute focused listening session" and the rule to do at least one step on a tune every day. — [Jazzadvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/how-to-learn-a-new-jazz-tune-every-day/)
- **John Cipolla** (Western Kentucky University handout, 2004), a chart-friendly approach:
  - Read the melody "exactly as it is written" at a slow tempo, with the metronome on 2 and 4.
  - Then learn a bass line and a guide-tone counter-melody.
  - "Keep the music in front of you until it begins to become second nature," and you'll look at it "less and less."

  — [Cipolla, "Learning Tunes," WKU](https://people.wku.edu/john.cipolla/Teaching_files/Learning%20Tunes.pdf)
- **Jazzadvice Trick 1 adds a structural pass from the record:** form, key centers, and Roman-numeral changes, before the details. — [Jazzadvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/3-tricks-to-learning-tunes-from-recordings-that-have-nothing-to-do-with-practicing/)

### Inferences
**A consolidated process for this user.** It reconciles LIST with the goal of reading from the 557:
1. **Listen.** Use 2–3 versions chosen as in (a): one straight vocal or original, one simple clear instrumental, optionally one favourite. Listen over several days, away from the horn, at least once with full attention.
2. **Learn the lyrics** (Songbook tunes) and **sing the melody** along with the record, then alone. Mark where phrases start and how long they are.
3. **On tenor, phrase by phrase** in 2–4-bar chunks:
   - Sing the phrase, find its first note on the horn, then play it back.
   - Use YouTube at 75% to check.
   - Whether to play along is a matter of taste: Vaartstra says no, Metcalf says yes. A reasonable split is to work it out without the record and then confirm along with it.
4. **Open the 557 (Bb) chart** and read the melody while the recording plays, checking spots where your ear and the page disagree. Fake-book melodies are usually the straight version, so differences often show where a performer embellished.
5. **Play the head from the chart** along with the recording or a backing track, so the band skill (reading and keeping your place) is what gets trained. Your ear now tells you whether what you read sounds right.

**Tenor octave choice:** pick the octave where the head sits comfortably and matches the record's register, usually low or middle on tenor. No source covered this, so it is my inference.

**How long it takes:** no source gives a figure. The educators frame it as days to weeks of background listening and a daily touch on the tune.
