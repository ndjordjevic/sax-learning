# Educator Methods for Learning a Jazz Standard (compared)

Research date: 2026-11-18. Scope: named step-by-step methods from jazz educators and institutions, for a beginner-to-intermediate tenor player who plays weekly with an amateur band reading from a fake book (the 557).

Methods actually retrieved and read (primary or near-primary text):
1. **Brent Vaartstra / LearnJazzStandards: LIST(S)**, from the free PDF *Learn Jazz Standards the Smart Way* (c. 2018, by upload path), plus the blog post "How To Learn A Jazz Standard And Not Forget It" (13 Jun 2015) and "Why You Should Learn The Lyrics" (11 Jan 2017).
2. **Jamey Aebersold: "Tips for Learning a New Tune"** (13 steps) and "Practice Procedures for Memorizing Scales and Chords to Any Song" (12 steps), both in the free *Jazz Handbook* (© 2000/2010/2013/2017).
3. **Hal Galper: "How To Learn A Tune"** (addendum dated 07/06/2012).
4. **Jazzadvice: "How to Learn a New Jazz Tune Every Day"** (author "Eric", undated).
5. **Bob Reynolds: "The Woodshed: Focus on the Framework"**, *Berklee Today*, Spring 2016.
6. **Michelle Mailhot: "6 Steps to Learning a New Jazz Tune"** (Clearwater Jazz handout, PDF dated 4 Aug 2020). Written for singers but instructive.
7. **Open Studio (Peter Martin and Adam Maness), "How to Learn New Tunes"** podcast episode, outline only.

## Q1: What exact steps do the well-known methods prescribe?

### Takeaway
Four fully documented methods (Vaartstra LIST, Aebersold 13 Tips, Galper, Jazzadvice) agree on the core: **listen many times, sing the melody, learn melody and changes (ideally by ear), check against paper, then improvise from memory**. They split on order. Vaartstra and Aebersold are ear-first and melody-first. Galper is structure-first and melody-last.

### Cited Findings

**Vaartstra, LIST(S): Listen, Internalize, Sing, Transfer (+ Study)**
- **L, Listen:** "put your instrument away and just listen. Find as many recordings as possible of the jazz standard you want to learn and go through all of them." Pro tip 1: learn the lyrics, which are "extremely helpful for internalizing melodies." — [LJS, Learn Jazz Standards the Smart Way (PDF)](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Learn-Jazz-Standards-the-Smart-Way.pdf)
- **I, Internalize:** more listening, but "intentional listening": full attention, no distractions, like watching a film. — [same PDF](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Learn-Jazz-Standards-the-Smart-Way.pdf)
- **S, Sing:** be able to sing the melody "both along with the recording and on your own before you learn it on your instrument". Singing "takes away 50% of the learning process." Advanced option: sing the bass roots to start hearing the harmony. Pro tip 2: "Frank Sinatra is your friend. Frank almost always sings melodies straight." For less classic songs, find the composer's or original artist's version. — [same PDF](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Learn-Jazz-Standards-the-Smart-Way.pdf)
- **T, Transfer:** only now pick up the instrument ("you shouldn't have been touching it until now"). (1) Transfer the melody you can already sing. (2) Learn the chords by ear, combining theory with ear training to identify chord quality and function. Pro tip 3: identify the bass note, then the chord quality. If you are stuck, look at the sheet music *while listening to a recording*, starting and stopping. — [same PDF](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Learn-Jazz-Standards-the-Smart-Way.pdf)
- **Bonus S, Study:** analyze the progression with Roman numerals to understand chord function, "If you can understand the theory, it will make improvising that much easier." This is drawn from *The Jazz Standards Playbook*. — [same PDF](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Learn-Jazz-Standards-the-Smart-Way.pdf)
- The earlier 2015 blog version gives 6 tips: listen first, learn from recordings not a fakebook ("Just because it's on a piece of paper doesn't mean it's correct"), sing before you play, learn melody AND harmony, choose tunes you like, keep playing it at jams and gigs. — [LJS blog, 13 Jun 2015](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/how-to-learn-a-jazz-standard/)

**Aebersold, "Tips For Learning A New Tune"** (*Jazz Handbook*, p. 9, quoted closely)
1. Listen to the song over and over.
2. Memorize the melody in your mind. Be able to sing it.
3. Listen carefully to the bass line and the harmony in general, to get an overall sense of how the song is put together.
4. Try playing the melody from memory, slowly at first.
5. Then play the melody along with the recording. Copy inflections, articulations, slurs, phrasing, dynamics.
6. Learn the scales and chords in the order they appear in the song. Get the right changes "from a reliable source, such as the play-a-long books."
7. Improvise over the harmony, "keeping in mind the original melody as a frame of reference."
8. Emphasize the thirds and sevenths in your soloing.
9. Memorize both melody and chords/scales. "Know where the chord tones are ON YOUR INSTRUMENT."
10. Improvise original melodies based on what your mind HEARS.
11. Listen constantly to the original recording and incorporate ideas from it into your solos.
12. Learn the lyrics if there are any. "Mentally sing the lyrics while playing the melody."
13. Fall in love with the melodies. "Play them like YOU wrote them."
— [Aebersold Jazz Handbook (free PDF, jazzbooks.com)](https://www.jazzbooks.com/mm5/download/FQBK-handbook.pdf)

**Aebersold, "Practice Procedures for Memorizing Scales and Chords to Any Song"** (the harmonic drill, done through the tune in order): (1) root of each chord, (2) first 2 scale notes, (3) first 3, (4) first 5, (5) triad 1-3-5, (6) 7th chord 1-3-5-7, (7) 9th chord, (8) whole scale up and down, (9) 6th chord, (10) scale up to the 9th and down the chord tones, (11) up the 9th chord and down the scale, (12) scale in broken thirds. Use a metronome if the recording is too fast. Vary the rhythms so it sounds improvised. Concentrate on the SOUND of each note. The worked example is Bb blues, thought of as three 4-bar sections. Once you have the scales and chords in your vocabulary, "you won't need to go through this practice procedure when working on a new tune." — [Aebersold Jazz Handbook](https://www.jazzbooks.com/mm5/download/FQBK-handbook.pdf)
- The Handbook names starter tunes: "blues in Bb or F, Satin Doll, Maiden Voyage, or Summertime." — [Aebersold Jazz Handbook](https://www.jazzbooks.com/mm5/download/FQBK-handbook.pdf)
- Aebersold also publishes David Baker's *How to Learn Tunes* (Vol. 76), "a quick and easy method for learning and memorizing melodies and chord changes to any tune in any key," using play-along tracks to train recognition of common harmonic formulas and song forms by eye and ear. The retailer page does not list the steps, and the level is Intermediate/Advanced. — [jazzbooks.com V76](https://jazzbooks.com/jazz/product/V76ES)

**Hal Galper, "How To Learn A Tune"** (principle: learn the simplest elements first and the melody last)
- Order: key, then meter, then length, then form (AABA, ABA...), then harmonic form (locate the major and minor I chords, each preceded by its II-V), then the first chord of each section (is it a I, II or V?), then melody, "It must be learned by rote. No short-cuts." Test: sing the melody with the chords played underneath, then without. (The numbered list on the site skips #3.) — [halgalper.com](https://www.halgalper.com/?p=647)
- Galper calls it "almost impossible" to memorize a tune in isolation in a practice room, and says it takes at least three performances to learn a tune. — [halgalper.com](https://www.halgalper.com/?p=647)
- Paper: use it the first two times through, then "throw it away" the third time and note what you couldn't remember. "When you're looking you're not hearing!" Review the tune in bed before sleep, going through its aspects in the order above, then work on the gaps the next day. — [halgalper.com](https://www.halgalper.com/?p=647)
- The 2012 addendum concedes it is really "How I Learn A Tune" and that methods vary with each player's ear and memory. — [halgalper.com](https://www.halgalper.com/?p=647)

**Jazzadvice, 3 steps** ("How to Learn a New Jazz Tune Every Day")
1. **Listen**, "with the intent to learn": one clear recording, focusing in turn on melody, chords, bass, drums. Sing the melody until confident.
2. **Figure out the details:** key (major or minor) and first chord; break the melody into intervals and chord tones; find the bass/root movement and progression; see how the melody fits the harmony; check on piano.
3. **Interpret:** compare several recordings, then choose your own tempo, feel and mood.
- Done as a daily habit with a list of 5 to 20 tunes, at least one step per tune per day, not as a cram session. — [Jazzadvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/how-to-learn-a-new-jazz-tune-every-day/)

**Bob Reynolds (Berklee Today, Spring 2016), framework drills**
- Put play-alongs away, since they "may hinder your learning of tunes from the ground up". Use a metronome at about 80 bpm instead.
- Play only the roots in time for three choruses to learn the form.
- "Think it. Say it. Play it.": say the roots aloud in time (example: F blues), then thirds, then triads, then 7th chords. Hesitation shows what to practice.
- Follow the 3rds and 7ths, looking for 7th-to-3rd resolutions (guide tones, though he doesn't use the term).
— [Berklee, "The Woodshed: Focus on the Framework"](https://www.berklee.edu/node/2339871)
- Reynolds's paid Virtual Studio has an *Autumn Leaves* lesson, "How to Memorize a Standard and Keep Your Place in the Form," built on guide tones. Its steps are behind a paywall. — [Bob Reynolds Virtual Studio](https://lessons.bobreynoldsmusic.com/learn-and-remember-tunes)
- He frames transcribing as "learning music by ear, from recordings, one phrase at a time," not notation, and encourages memorizing whenever possible. — [bobreynoldsmusic.com](https://bobreynoldsmusic.com/memorize-music-in-a-hurry)

**Michelle Mailhot, "6 Steps to Learning a New Jazz Tune"** (2020, vocal-oriented): First, listen to at least 6 versions. (1) What key do you sing it in? (2) Analyze the form: total bars, bars per section, AABA or ABAC. (3) Melodic and harmonic analysis: scale degrees of the melody against the chords; sing the roots, then add 3rds, 5ths and 7ths. (4) Sing the melody without lyrics. (5) Sing it with lyrics, speak the words, and paraphrase the story. (6) Work in a typical intro and ending, keep the form in your head so you don't miss your entrance after solos, and practice with iReal Pro. — [Clearwater Jazz PDF](https://www.clearwaterjazz.com/_uploaded_files/6_steps_to_learning_a_new_tune.pdf)

**Open Studio (Peter Martin and Adam Maness), "How to Learn New Tunes"**: (1) Listen, (2) "Stay away from the pages (learn from recordings)", (3) "Play the heck out of it", (4) "Be patient (memorize it as you learn it)", (5) an "ULTIMATE TIP" that is not stated in the show notes. A separate episode, "4 Memory Hacks To Learn Standards" (S7 E46), exists, but its content was not retrieved. — [You'll Hear It: How to Learn New Tunes](https://youllhearit.transistor.fm/episodes/how-to-learn-new-tunes); [4 Memory Hacks](https://youllhearit.com/episodes/4-memory-hacks-to-learn-standards)

**Bill Cunliffe**: "First practice the melody slowly, with a metronome, until it's secure"; listen to several recorded versions and isolate the melody and harmony (chords and bass line). Post dated 21 Nov 2012; the rest of the steps are in a PDF that wasn't retrieved. — [billcunliffe.com](https://www.billcunliffe.com/blog-thoughts/tune-learning-8dxa4)

### Inferences
- The consensus skeleton is: (1) listen repeatedly, (2) sing the melody, (3) hear and know the bass/roots and the form, (4) play the melody from memory, (5) learn the changes (roots, then chord tones, then 3rds and 7ths), (6) improvise using the melody as a frame, (7) perform it repeatedly.
- Aebersold's 13 Tips is the most complete single horn-player checklist, and it is free. It also already covers lyrics, guide tones (3rds and 7ths) and copying articulation from the recording.

### Gaps
- **Jens Larsen**: no text version of his tune-learning steps was found. His YouTube video "How to learn a jazz standard – important exercises" exists, but no transcript was retrieved.
- **Barry Harris**: no primary source was found for his tune-learning method or for the widely repeated "learn the lyrics" advice attributed to him. Don't attribute it without a source.
- **Jerry Coker and Hal Crook**: only publisher blurbs were found. Crook's *How to Improvise* is a method for practicing improvisation, not tune learning, and no numbered tune-learning steps were found for Coker.
- **Chris Potter**: only secondhand notes from an NYU talk ("deconstructive learning": take something apart and reassemble it) — [bobreynoldsmusic.com](https://bobreynoldsmusic.com/chris-potter-nyu-interview/). Nothing tune-specific.
- **Juilliard and Berklee official curricula, Peter Spitzer, "Tune Up"/Jazz Path**: not retrieved within the time budget. The only Berklee-hosted item is the Reynolds article.

## Q2: Order of steps (listen vs read; melody vs chords; singing; lyrics; bass/roots; Roman numerals; guide tones; arpeggios; keys)

### Takeaway
Every method that addresses it puts **listening first** and **singing before playing**. Most go **melody before chords**. Galper is the clear outlier with structure first and melody last. Roots/bass come before full chord tones, and 3rds/7ths (guide tones) come at or just before the improvisation stage. All-keys practice is a late, optional step.

### Cited Findings
- **Listen before touching the horn:** Vaartstra says not to touch the instrument until the Transfer step — [LJS PDF](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Learn-Jazz-Standards-the-Smart-Way.pdf). Aebersold's step 1 is "Listen to the song over and over" — [Handbook](https://www.jazzbooks.com/mm5/download/FQBK-handbook.pdf). Jazzadvice's step 1 is Listen, and Open Studio's is "Listen" — [Jazzadvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/how-to-learn-a-new-jazz-tune-every-day/); [Open Studio](https://youllhearit.transistor.fm/episodes/how-to-learn-new-tunes). Mailhot says listen to at least 6 versions first — [Clearwater](https://www.clearwaterjazz.com/_uploaded_files/6_steps_to_learning_a_new_tune.pdf).
- **Melody first vs last:** Vaartstra and Aebersold do melody, then chords. Galper explicitly does key, meter, length, form and harmonic landmarks first, melody last — [halgalper.com](https://www.halgalper.com/?p=647). Mailhot does form and harmony analysis (steps 2–3) before singing the melody (steps 4–5) — [Clearwater](https://www.clearwaterjazz.com/_uploaded_files/6_steps_to_learning_a_new_tune.pdf).
- **Singing:** sing before playing (Vaartstra; Aebersold step 2). Galper's test is to sing the melody with and then without chords. Mailhot sings roots, then 3rds, 5ths and 7ths. Aebersold step 12 says to mentally sing the lyrics while playing.
- **Lyrics:** Vaartstra (Pro Tip 1) and Aebersold (step 12) say to learn them. Mailhot gives them a dedicated step. Jazzadvice's daily-tune article and Galper don't mention lyrics. Vaartstra's 2017 lyrics post argues they reveal the song's intent, shape phrasing like spoken sentences, and aid memorization, and quotes Dexter Gordon: "When you know the lyrics to a tune, you have some kind of insight as to it's composition." — [LJS lyrics post, 11 Jan 2017](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/?p=37824)
- **Bass line/roots:** sing the roots (Vaartstra, advanced); listen to the bass line (Aebersold step 3); find the root movement (Jazzadvice); play roots in time for 3 choruses, or say them aloud (Reynolds); start the drill with "Play root/tonic note of each chord" (Aebersold).
- **Roman-numeral or functional analysis:** Vaartstra's bonus "Study" step comes last — [LJS PDF](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Learn-Jazz-Standards-the-Smart-Way.pdf). Galper puts functional landmarks (I chords and their II-Vs, the first chord of each section) early, before the melody — [halgalper.com](https://www.halgalper.com/?p=647).
- **Guide tones:** Aebersold step 8, "Emphasize the thirds and sevenths". Reynolds, 7th-to-3rd resolutions — [Berklee](https://www.berklee.edu/node/2339871).
- **Arpeggiating:** Aebersold's 12-step drill goes root, then scale fragments, triad, 7th chord, 9th chord and full scale — [Handbook](https://www.jazzbooks.com/mm5/download/FQBK-handbook.pdf). Reynolds goes roots, then 3rds, triads, 7ths — [Berklee](https://www.berklee.edu/node/2339871).
- **Multiple keys:** Baker's Vol. 76 is pitched at learning tunes "in any key" — [jazzbooks.com](https://jazzbooks.com/jazz/product/V76ES). Galper and Vaartstra's LIST don't require transposition. A teacher's blog (via search snippet only) advises learning the whole tune in one key first, then other keys.

### Inferences
- Galper's "structure first" approach and the "ear first" approach can be combined. Even ear-first methods have you listen for the bass and form (Aebersold step 3) before you play. For a player with a fake book at the weekly band, knowing the form and section landmarks first is what prevents getting lost.
- For a beginner tenor player, all-keys work is low priority. No method treats it as part of first learning a tune.

### Gaps
- No retrieved source gives an empirical reason to prefer melody-first over structure-first. It is expert preference.

## Q3: Handling the melody: by ear from recordings vs lead sheet? Which recordings?

### Takeaway
There is strong consensus to learn the melody **by ear from recordings** and use the lead sheet only to **check** your work. Use a straight vocal reference (Sinatra) or the composer's or original version to find the "real" melody, and compare several versions. Aebersold and Galper allow paper more pragmatically: Aebersold for getting the right changes, Galper for the first two passes.

### Cited Findings
- "Jazz is not music meant to be learned from sheet music." Sheet music is fine "for checking the work your ear has done" — [LJS PDF](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Learn-Jazz-Standards-the-Smart-Way.pdf). Check against a fakebook afterward, because "Just because it's on a piece of paper doesn't mean it's correct" — [LJS 2015](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/how-to-learn-a-jazz-standard/).
- Which recordings: "Frank Sinatra is your friend" because he sings melodies straight; for less classic tunes, the original composer or artist — [LJS PDF](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Learn-Jazz-Standards-the-Smart-Way.pdf). The LJS lyrics post contrasts Sinatra (straight) with Billie Holiday (many liberties) — [LJS 2017](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/?p=37824). Jazzadvice suggests one clear recording to learn from, then several to compare for interpretation — [Jazzadvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/how-to-learn-a-new-jazz-tune-every-day/). Mailhot says at least 6 versions — [Clearwater](https://www.clearwaterjazz.com/_uploaded_files/6_steps_to_learning_a_new_tune.pdf).
- Aebersold: play the melody from memory, then *with the recording*, copying "inflections, articulations, slurs, phrasing, dynamics", and get the changes "from a reliable source, such as the play-a-long books" — [Handbook](https://www.jazzbooks.com/mm5/download/FQBK-handbook.pdf).
- Jazzadvice warns that if reading "the sheet music or a fake book" is your only step, you miss "the most important part of any tune" — [Jazzadvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/how-to-learn-a-new-jazz-tune-every-day/). Open Studio says "Stay away from the pages" — [Open Studio](https://youllhearit.transistor.fm/episodes/how-to-learn-new-tunes).
- Galper: the melody "must be learned by rote. No short-cuts". Paper is allowed for 2 passes only — [halgalper.com](https://www.halgalper.com/?p=647).

### Inferences
- For a band that reads from the 557, a practical hybrid fits the sources: learn the melody by ear from a straight vocal recording, then compare it with the 557 page, since the band will play the 557's changes. This follows Vaartstra's "check with paper" and Aebersold's "make sure you've got the right changes."

### Gaps
- No source here recommends specific recordings per tune for tenor players, beyond the Sinatra heuristic and the Aebersold Handbook's general "Historically Significant Recordings" list.

## Q4: Practising improvisation on a new tune

### Takeaway
The recommended ladder runs **roots, then chord tones (triads, 7ths), then 3rds and 7ths (guide tones), then scales, then improvising with the melody as the frame of reference**, plus borrowing ideas from the recordings and transcribing. Aebersold and Reynolds are the most concrete.

### Cited Findings
- Aebersold: improvise "keeping in mind the original melody as a frame of reference" (step 7), emphasize 3rds and 7ths (step 8), know the chord tones on your instrument (step 9), improvise what your mind hears (step 10), incorporate ideas from the original recording (step 11) — [Handbook](https://www.jazzbooks.com/mm5/download/FQBK-handbook.pdf). The same booklet says chord tones (1, 3, 5, 7) "are great notes to begin and end a phrase with", and that transcribing solos from recordings is "one of the best ways to find out what the professional is" doing — [Handbook](https://www.jazzbooks.com/mm5/download/FQBK-handbook.pdf).
- Reynolds: roots in time for 3 choruses, then say and play roots, 3rds, triads and 7ths, then 7th-to-3rd resolutions. Master the framework before playing outside — [Berklee 2016](https://www.berklee.edu/node/2339871).
- Vaartstra: Roman-numeral understanding "will make improvising that much easier" — [LJS PDF](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Learn-Jazz-Standards-the-Smart-Way.pdf).
- Jazzadvice: aim to tell a musical story rather than just play the right notes. Interpretation comes after the tune is internalized — [Jazzadvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/how-to-learn-a-new-jazz-tune-every-day/).
- Tool disagreement: Aebersold's system is built on play-alongs and recommends them as the reliable source of changes — [Handbook](https://www.jazzbooks.com/mm5/download/FQBK-handbook.pdf). Reynolds says to put play-alongs away when learning a tune and use a metronome — [Berklee](https://www.berklee.edu/node/2339871). Mailhot recommends iReal Pro — [Clearwater](https://www.clearwaterjazz.com/_uploaded_files/6_steps_to_learning_a_new_tune.pdf).

### Inferences
- A beginner-appropriate order that fits all the sources: (1) roots in time, (2) arpeggios through the form, (3) guide-tone line (3rds and 7ths), (4) embellish the melody, (5) free improvising with chord tones on strong beats. Licks and transcribed phrases are added from the tune's own recordings.

### Gaps
- None of the retrieved methods prescribes "learn licks" as a tune-learning step. The closest is Aebersold's "incorporate ideas off the recording." Melody-embellishment as a first improvisation step is implied by Aebersold step 7 but not spelled out here.

## Q5: Memorizing; not reading at gigs or jam sessions

### Takeaway
Every source wants the tune memorized. Galper and Open Studio are the most anti-paper. The mechanisms are repetition in performance (Galper: 3 or more performances; Vaartstra: call it at jams), memorizing as you go, spaced daily contact (Jazzadvice), and mental review away from the horn (Galper).

### Cited Findings
- Galper: you can't really memorize a tune in a practice room alone. It takes 3 or more performances. Ditch the paper on the third pass. "When you're looking you're not hearing!" Review mentally before sleep — [halgalper.com](https://www.halgalper.com/?p=647).
- Vaartstra: the common pitfalls are "You can't play a jazz standard without looking at sheet music" and getting lost in the form. The cure is the LIST process — [LJS PDF](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/Learn-Jazz-Standards-the-Smart-Way.pdf). Keep playing the tune and call it at jam sessions and gigs; choose tunes you like so they stick — [LJS 2015](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/how-to-learn-a-jazz-standard/).
- Aebersold: "Memorize everything you can". Memorizing melodies, scales and chords "gives courage to your imagination" — [Handbook](https://www.jazzbooks.com/mm5/download/FQBK-handbook.pdf).
- Jazzadvice: against cramming. Absorb the tune through repeated listening across days — [Jazzadvice](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/how-to-learn-a-new-jazz-tune-every-day/).
- Open Studio: "Be patient (memorize it as you learn it)" — [Open Studio](https://youllhearit.transistor.fm/episodes/how-to-learn-new-tunes).
- Reynolds: memorize whenever possible; he memorized 20+ Snarky Puppy tunes in about a week by breaking the task down — [bobreynoldsmusic.com](https://bobreynoldsmusic.com/memorize-music-in-a-hurry); [bobreynoldsmusic.com, 20 songs](https://bobreynoldsmusic.com/how-i-memorized-20-snarky-puppy-songs-in-one-week/).
- Mailhot: keep the form in your head so you don't miss your entrance after solos — [Clearwater](https://www.clearwaterjazz.com/_uploaded_files/6_steps_to_learning_a_new_tune.pdf).

### Inferences
- For a band that reads from a fake book, Galper's "paper twice, then away" fits naturally. Read from the 557 at the first rehearsals, then try the head and form from memory and check the page only where you got stuck. This makes the weekly band the "performance context" Galper says memorization needs.

### Gaps
- No retrieved source talks specifically about amateur bands where everyone reads. The advice assumes jam sessions and gigs.
- The content of Open Studio's "4 Memory Hacks" episode was not retrieved.

## Summary: where the methods agree and disagree

**Agreement:** listen many times before playing; sing the melody; know the bass/roots and form; melody and harmony are both required; use paper as a check, not the primary source; memorize; repetition and performance make tunes stick; target 3rds and 7ths when improvising.

**Disagreement:**
1. Melody-first (Vaartstra, Aebersold, Jazzadvice) vs structure-first, melody-last (Galper; Mailhot puts analysis before melody).
2. Strict "don't touch the horn yet" (Vaartstra) vs playing along with the recording early (Aebersold step 5).
3. Play-alongs as the source of changes and a practice tool (Aebersold; iReal Pro in Mailhot) vs put play-alongs away and use a metronome (Reynolds 2016).
4. Paper use: never, learn from recordings (Open Studio, LJS ideal) vs paper for the first 2 passes (Galper) vs "a reliable source, such as the play-a-long books" for changes (Aebersold).
5. Lyrics: explicit steps for Vaartstra, Aebersold and Mailhot; not mentioned by Galper or Jazzadvice's daily-tune article.
6. Theory/Roman numerals: a late bonus (Vaartstra) vs early functional landmarks (Galper).
