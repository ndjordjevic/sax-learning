# Learning science and music cognition applied to learning jazz standards

Scope: what peer-reviewed music-psychology research (plus clearly labelled practitioner and anecdotal sources) says about learning, memorizing and keeping tunes (melody and changes), and about building improvisation fluency. It is aimed at an adult amateur tenor player learning standards for a weekly band.

Evidence-strength key used below: **[Strong]** means replicated or controlled studies. **[Moderate]** means small controlled studies or a consistent run of case studies. **[Weak/Case]** means a single case study or correlational data. **[Anecdote]** means practitioner or ethnographic testimony.

---

## 1. Memorization of music: aural, visual, kinesthetic and conceptual memory, and the role of analysis and structure

### Takeaway
Across classical and jazz case studies, expert memorizers use the tune's **formal structure as a retrieval scheme** and practise **"performance cues"** (landmarks) until those cues automatically call up the next passage. Rote "serial-chain" memory (one bar triggers the next) is fast to build but fragile: lapses cluster at section boundaries. Conceptual or structural knowledge is what lets a player recover or jump in mid-tune. For a jazz standard this means memorizing the **form plus the harmonic road-map** deliberately, not only playing the head until the fingers know it.

### Cited Findings
- Chaffin & Imreh (2002, *Psychological Science* 13(4)) followed concert pianist Gabriela Imreh learning Bach's Italian Concerto Presto. The formal structure and her performance cues affected what she practised most, where she hesitated when first playing from memory, and what she recalled 2 years later. The authors concluded she "used the formal structure as a retrieval scheme and performance cues as retrieval cues," in line with expert-memory principles (Ericsson). [Moderate: single case, but very detailed and later replicated in other case studies] — [Chaffin & Imreh 2002 PDF](https://musiclab.uconn.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/290/2013/10/Practicing-Perfection-Chaffin-2002.pdf)
- Cue types in that framework: **basic** (familiar patterns, fingering, technical difficulties), **interpretive** (phrasing, dynamics, tempo), **expressive** (the emotion to convey) and **structural** (section boundaries and "switches", the places where similar passages diverge). At switches, the approach "does not provide cues about which way to continue," so "a conceptual representation of the formal structure had to" be used. — [Chaffin & Imreh 2002 PDF](https://musiclab.uconn.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/290/2013/10/Practicing-Perfection-Chaffin-2002.pdf)
- **Jazz-specific replication:** Noice, Jeffrey, Noice & Chaffin (2008, *Psychology of Music* 36(1):63–79) videotaped an experienced jazz pianist (20+ years professional) learning Hank Mobley's *Funk in Deep Freeze* (AABA) without ever hearing it. His process "was similar to that reported for classical musicians. That is, he used the musical structure as a retrieval scheme and practiced using performance cues." He started practice segments at subsection beginnings 12.5 times more often than at other bars, and he analysed the chord progressions as an explicit memorization step (for example, spotting a cycle of fourths with inner tritones). Caveat: the study covered only the note-for-note head, not the improvised choruses. [Weak/Case, n=1, but directly on jazz] — [Noice et al. 2008 (UConn Music Lab PDF)](https://production.wordpress.uconn.edu/musiclab/wp-content/uploads/sites/290/2013/10/MemoryJazzMusician.pdf)
- In the same paper the authors speculate that "a mental map of this sort may be essential for many aspects of jazz improvisation," letting the player "keep track of where he is in the piece and... decide where to go next." This is labelled as speculation. — [Noice et al. 2008](https://production.wordpress.uconn.edu/musiclab/wp-content/uploads/sites/290/2013/10/MemoryJazzMusician.pdf)
- Jane Ginsborg distinguishes **serial cueing** (each passage triggers the next, built through rote repetition, "easily disrupted" by distraction) from **content-addressable memory** (retrieving from landmarks such as section boundaries, so you can jump straight in). A re-analysis of her data found that "lapses happen at section boundaries" and that recoveries also tend to happen there. Chunks should be meaningful to the individual: she and her pianist husband divided the same piece into seven sections and four sections respectively. [Moderate, interview summary of her research] — [Bulletproof Musician: Jane Ginsborg on musical memory](https://bulletproofmusician.com/jane-ginsborg-on-building-stronger-and-more-reliable-musical-memory/)
- Musicians also describe memory as embodied (eyes, hands and fingers "knowing" the passage). This is the kinesthetic component, and it is useful but it is the part most vulnerable under pressure. — [Bulletproof Musician: Ginsborg](https://bulletproofmusician.com/jane-ginsborg-on-building-stronger-and-more-reliable-musical-memory/)
- Ginsborg's observational study of 15 singers over up to six sessions: the fastest, most accurate memorizers were not the ones who made the fewest mistakes, and experience did not predict success. What did distinguish them was setting goals from the start, monitoring progress, catching and correcting errors, learning in sections and then chaining the sections. [Moderate] — [Bulletproof Musician: Ginsborg](https://bulletproofmusician.com/jane-ginsborg-on-building-stronger-and-more-reliable-musical-memory/); see also [Classical singers learning and memorising a new song (Scilit record)](https://www.scilit.net/publications/02633ccded41bd8746255638809e4c9c)
- Ginsborg's 2024 open-access chapter *Memory for Music: Research and Practice for Performers* sets out research-based memorization recommendations for performers and teachers. I could not retrieve its full text. — [Open Book Publishers ch. 11](https://www.openbookpublishers.com/books/10.11647/obp.0389/chapters/10.11647/obp.0389.11)

### Inferences
- For a standard, the "formal structure" is the form (AABA or ABAC, 32 bars or a 12-bar blues) plus the harmonic plan (key areas, ii–V's, turnarounds). Naming these explicitly ("A = I–vi–ii–V, bridge goes to IV", "bar 5 is the first switch between A1 and A2") supplies the retrieval scheme the studies describe.
- Practise starting from section and phrase beginnings, not only from bar 1. This builds content-addressable entry points, so after a mistake on the bandstand you can come back in at the next section.
- Mark the A1/A2 "switch" points (different endings or turnarounds) as deliberate cues. They are where the case studies show hesitations.

### Gaps
- No controlled experiment I found directly compares "aural vs visual vs kinesthetic vs analytic" memorization strategies head-to-head for retention. The evidence for analysis comes mostly from expert case studies and correlations.
- I could not locate a Mishra meta-analysis on memorization or mental practice. Searches for "Mishra meta-analysis mental practice" returned nothing verifiable ([search hits returned Platz et al. 2014 and Mielke & Comeau 2017 instead](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4073287)). Mishra's memorization work therefore isn't cited here.
- Lehmann's memorization work appears only second-hand (see section 6).

## 2. Learning by ear vs from notation (ear-playing and improvisation)

### Takeaway
In McPherson's studies, **playing by ear is the "hub" skill**: it was the only one of five performance skills that correlated with all the others, and it exerted a direct influence on improvising, sight-reading and playing from memory. Heavy notation dependence is linked to weaker pitch recall by ear. Jazz tradition ("learn it off the record") agrees, though that part of the evidence is ethnographic or anecdotal.

### Cited Findings
- McPherson (1995) and McPherson, Bailey & Sinclair (1997) studied 101 high-school instrumentalists. "Playing by ear was the only attribute that correlated with the other four attributes" (sight-reading, playing from memory, improvising, performing rehearsed music). [Moderate, correlational/path model] — [Corcoran & Spiro 2021 review (RCM)](https://researchonline.rcm.ac.uk/id/eprint/1919/1/Corcoran%20%26%20Spiro%202021.pdf); [dspace.mic.ul.ie thesis summarizing McPherson](https://dspace.mic.ul.ie/bitstreams/950fad9a-8780-429c-ac3a-7feeceff73c4/download)
- Path analysis in this work: ear playing "exerted direct influence on improvising, sight reading and playing from memory and an indirect influence on performing rehearsed music" (McPherson 1995b; McPherson, Bailey & Sinclair 1997; McPherson & Gabrielsson 2002). — [dspace.mic.ul.ie](https://dspace.mic.ul.ie/bitstreams/950fad9a-8780-429c-ac3a-7feeceff73c4/download)
- McPherson's definition of playing by ear: "the ability to reproduce on a musical instrument an existing passage or piece of music, which has been learnt aurally," at the original pitch or transposed. — [dspace.mic.ul.ie](https://dspace.mic.ul.ie/bitstreams/950fad9a-8780-429c-ac3a-7feeceff73c4/download)
- Woody & Lehmann (2010) compared formally trained ("classical") musicians with musicians who had vernacular (pop or folk) experience on ear-playing tasks. The citing literature reports that vernacular experience was linked to better ear-playing. Corcoran & Spiro (2021) argue that "score-dependency" is associated with difficulty reproducing **pitch** (but not rhythm) content by ear. [Moderate] — [Corcoran & Spiro 2021](https://researchonline.rcm.ac.uk/id/eprint/1919/1/Corcoran%20%26%20Spiro%202021.pdf)
- Woody's framing: in ear playing, the pitches and rhythms played are "informed by an inner hearing" (audiation). — [Corcoran & Spiro 2021](https://researchonline.rcm.ac.uk/id/eprint/1919/1/Corcoran%20%26%20Spiro%202021.pdf)
- A replication on musical sophistication found that "Playing by Ear" was predicted by Gold-MSI singing-ability subscales and improvising by "Active Engagement", while sight-reading had no significant predictors. — [summary in search results; source repository](https://minerva-access.unimelb.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/9cc8eee9-b78d-500a-b164-828edb9384ed/content)
- Berliner's *Thinking in Jazz* (1994), an ethnography drawing on interviews with dozens of professionals, describes learning from recordings as analogous to learning a mother tongue (chapter "Getting Your Vocabulary Straight"). [Anecdote/ethnographic, but the standard scholarly account of jazz pedagogy] — [Erling Aksdal, "Learning the Jazz Language" (discussing Berliner)](https://erlingak.folk.ntnu.no/LearningJazzLanguage/LearningJazzLanguage.pdf); [Chicago UP book record](https://dx.doi.org/10.7208/chicago/9780226044521.001.0001)
- Practitioner claim (not research): learning tunes from records means "subconsciously gathering musical techniques", and lead sheets keep "a separation between you and the sound of the music." [Anecdote] — [LearnJazzStandards: learn jazz by ear](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/?p=40479); [JazzAdvice: why learn by ear](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/why-you-should-learn-music-by-ear/)

### Inferences
- The strongest evidence-backed lever for improvisation and memory alike is ear-playing practice. For each standard, learning the melody from a recording (with the lead sheet as a check, not as the source) trains the hub skill.
- For an advanced beginner a hybrid is realistic: listen many times, sing it, play it by ear, then check against the 557/Bb lead sheet. This keeps pitch audiation in the loop, which is the weakness that score-dependency is linked to.

### Gaps
- No study found that measures learning speed or retention of a jazz head by ear vs from notation in adult amateurs. McPherson's data are on school-age players and are correlational, so causality is inferred.
- The full Woody & Lehmann 2010 effect sizes were not retrieved.

## 3. Spacing, interleaving vs blocked practice, retrieval practice, mental practice and sleep, applied to musicians

### Takeaway
The general learning-science effects mostly carry over, but the **music-specific evidence is thinner and noisier** than in verbal learning. Interleaving has small positive music studies. Sleep consolidation of newly learned melodies is well supported. Spacing is supported in general skill learning but had a null result in one piano-sequence study. Mental practice and singing alone are weaker than playing, while **combined** imagery, singing and playing beat playing alone on expressiveness in one study.

### Cited Findings
**Interleaving / contextual interference**
- Carter & Grahn (2016, *Frontiers in Psychology*): 10 advanced clarinetists, within-subjects. Two pieces were practised blocked (12 minutes each) and two interleaved (3-minute segments alternating). Interleaved scores were "consistently higher but not statistically significant" when averaged across raters, with some rater-specific significant effects (e.g. Wilcoxon p = 0.02). Blocked technical-study performance declined from Day 1 to Day 2. Eight of 10 participants commented favourably on interleaving's effects on goal-setting, focus and error detection. The limitations are serious: n=10, inconsistent raters (r = 0.02–0.59) and only 12 minutes per piece. [Weak–Moderate] — [Carter & Grahn 2016 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4989027)
- Earlier music studies cited there: Stambaugh (2009), where beginner clarinetists who interleaved played faster at retention; Stambaugh & Demorest (2010), with 7th-grade clarinet and **saxophone** players, which found no effect on technical accuracy; and Stambaugh (2011a), where the effect held for university woodwinds but not brass. — [Carter & Grahn 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4989027)
- Steenstrup et al. (2021): among 50 trumpet students, self-reported frequent random (interleaved) practice correlated with greater pitch improvement. This was exploratory and uncorrected. — [Steenstrup et al. 2021, Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.757052/full)

**Spacing**
- Rubin-Rabson (1940) had pianists learn music with 1-hour vs 2-day lags between learning episodes and is commonly cited as showing a spacing benefit. I could only access it through secondary citation. — [Wiseheart et al. 2017 discussion (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5553926)
- **Counter-evidence:** Wiseheart et al. (2017, *PLOS ONE*), "Lack of spacing effects during piano learning": no spacing benefit when novices learned a 17-note piano sequence. The authors note "relatively little evidence" exists on spacing for complex motor-skill retention. — [Wiseheart et al. 2017 (IDEAS/PLOS ONE)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/plo/pone00/0182986.html); [PMC5553926](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5553926)
- Smith & Scarf (2017, *Frontiers*) review: spaced practice "generally leads to better learning and retention than massed practice" for skill learning. — [Frontiers 2017 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00962/pdf)
- The Chaffin & Imreh case shows naturally spaced learning: about 11.5 hours in 12 sessions over 4 weeks, then a 15-week break, then 8 hours in 12 sessions over 2 weeks, then a further period, for a total of about 33.5 hours over 57 sessions spread across 10 months. [Case description, not a spacing test] — [Chaffin & Imreh 2002](https://musiclab.uconn.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/290/2013/10/Practicing-Perfection-Chaffin-2002.pdf)

**Sleep consolidation**
- Simmons & Duke (2006, *JRME* 54(3):257–269): music students learned a short (12-note) keyboard melody with the non-dominant hand. Performance improved after a night's sleep rather than after an equal waking interval. A secondary source puts the sample at about 75 UT-Austin music majors (this figure is not verified against the paper). [Moderate–Strong; consistent with the wider motor-sequence sleep literature] — [UT Austin CML: skill learning and procedural memory](https://cml.music.utexas.edu/research/skill-learning-and-procedural-memory); [Bulletproof Musician on sleep](https://bulletproofmusician.com/heres-an-easy-way-to-help-your-brain-learn-faster/)
- Allen (Simmons) follow-up: participants who learned **one** melody in the evening showed overnight gains, but those who learned the target melody **plus** a second melody showed none (interference). A brief re-test of melody A after learning melody B "seems to have reactivated" A and offset the interference. — [Procedural memory consolidation in musicians (TDL thesis)](https://tdl-ir.tdl.org/items/63f63d23-337b-441c-abae-ba630b03d571/full)
- Finger-tapping sequence research generally reports about 10–13% faster performance with fewer errors after sleep. — [MedicalNewsToday release on musician sleep study](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/releases/259155); [news-medical.net 2013](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20130416/New-study-examines-impact-of-sleep-on-learning-process-for-musicians.aspx)

**Mental practice and singing (see also section 6)**
- Steenstrup et al. (2021): 50 academy trumpet students each tried physical practice, mental imagery, singing, combined (sing + play + imagine) and no practice on short etudes. **Physical practice and combined** improved overall performance significantly more than no practice. **Singing alone and imagery alone did not.** **Only the combined strategy** significantly improved musical expression. Prior solfège training correlated with fewer pitch errors. Limitations: about 3-minute interventions, advanced players, immediate test only. [Moderate] — [Steenstrup et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.757052/full)
- Mielke & Comeau (2017) reviewed 33 studies of mental practice in music and found inconsistent terminology and methods, which limits firm conclusions. — [Mielke & Comeau 2017 (uOttawa PDF)](https://piano.uottawa.ca/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/Publications/Music%20Reading%20%20Learning%20a%20new%20skill/34.%20Mielke%20_Gomeau%20Mental%20practice%202017.pdf)
- Ginsborg calls holding the music in mind and rehearsing it in imagination "really important". This is an expert opinion. — [Bulletproof Musician: Ginsborg](https://bulletproofmusician.com/jane-ginsborg-on-building-stronger-and-more-reliable-musical-memory/)

**Retrieval practice**
- The Chaffin & Imreh and Noice studies both show experts deliberately performing from memory ("memory runs") and repeatedly starting at cue points. That is retrieval practice in all but name, and hesitations in those runs revealed the weak spots. — [Chaffin & Imreh 2002](https://musiclab.uconn.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/290/2013/10/Practicing-Perfection-Chaffin-2002.pdf); [Noice et al. 2008](https://production.wordpress.uconn.edu/musiclab/wp-content/uploads/sites/290/2013/10/MemoryJazzMusician.pdf)

### Inferences
- Rotate two or three tunes in a session (short interleaved blocks) rather than drilling one for 30 minutes. The music evidence is small but points the same way, and it costs nothing.
- Learn a new head in the evening and avoid learning a second new melody straight afterwards. If you must, briefly replay the first one at the end of the session.
- After the first exposure, test from memory early (play or sing the head and changes without the chart), then check the chart. Treat hesitations as the cue map for the next session.
- Mental rehearsal and singing are best used as **additions** to playing (for example, away from the horn, or as a warm-up step), not as replacements.
- Spread a tune over several short sessions on different days rather than one long one. General skill-learning evidence supports this even though one piano study was null.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found on retrieval practice ("testing effect") specifically for music memorization. The support is indirect, from expert case studies and the general learning literature, which I did not fetch here.
- No music-specific study found that tests optimal spacing intervals (for example an Anki-style expanding schedule) for tune retention.

## 4. Chunking of chord progressions and schema theory in jazz (Pressing, Berliner, Norgaard)

### Takeaway
Improvisers draw heavily on **stored, pre-learned patterns** (a "knowledge base"), guided by a tune-specific **referent** (Pressing). Corpus and dual-task studies by Norgaard show that most notes in Charlie Parker's solos begin recurring four-interval patterns, and that improvisers lean on such patterns more when attention is taxed. Patterns therefore act as cognitive chunks that free attention. Treating ii–V–I and turnarounds as chunks is the theoretically consistent way to learn changes, though no study directly tests ii–V chunk learning.

### Cited Findings
- Norgaard (2014, *Music Perception* 31(3)): computer analysis of 48 Charlie Parker solos found that **82.6% of notes begin a four-interval pattern** that recurs elsewhere, which the author reads as evidence that "pre-formed structures are inserted during improvisation." [Moderate, corpus study] — [Norgaard 2014 (CORE PDF)](https://core.ac.uk/download/71427376.pdf); [GSU ScholarWorks](https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/music_facpub/78)
- Norgaard, Emerson, Dawn & Fidlon (2016, *Music Perception* 33(5)): skilled jazz pianists improvising under a secondary counting task used **significantly more repeated patterns**. Stored patterns appear to reduce attentional load. [Moderate, experimental] — [GSU ScholarWorks](https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/music_facpub/79); [UT CML jazz cognition](https://cml.music.utexas.edu/research/cognition-in-jazz-improvisation)
- Norgaard, Bales & Hansen (2023, *Cognition*): in about 100,000 notes from one pianist compared with 24 advanced pianists, the pattern library "may be idiosyncratic to the individual improviser." — [search summary; Clarivate record](https://clarivate.forskningsportal.dk/display/pub-wos-000885281100002)
- Norgaard (2011, *JRME* 59:109–127): 7 artist-level jazz musicians improvised over a blues with a drum track, then described their thinking while viewing a notation of their solo. This produced a coding frame of expert improvisational thinking that later studies of developing improvisers were compared against (Norgaard 2017). [Moderate, qualitative] — [UT repository (dissertation)](https://repositories.lib.utexas.edu/handle/2152/17893?show=full); [CML lab](https://cml.music.utexas.edu/research/cognition-in-jazz-improvisation)
- Pressing (1988) modelled improvisation as events linked by congruent or incongruent ideas, drawing on a **knowledge base** (materials, patterns, excerpts) and a **referent**, "an underlying formal scheme or guiding image specific to a given piece... used... to facilitate the generation and editing of improvised behaviour." In standards the referent is essentially the tune and its changes. [Theory] — [Frontiers 2017 (citing Pressing)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00966/pdf); [Smither 2024, Music Theory Online](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.24.30.3/mto.24.30.3.smither.html)
- Noice et al. (2008): the jazz pianist explicitly analysed chord progressions as a memorization step and noticed harmonic sub-structures (a cycle of fourths). Harmonic chunking therefore served memory directly. — [Noice et al. 2008](https://production.wordpress.uconn.edu/musiclab/wp-content/uploads/sites/290/2013/10/MemoryJazzMusician.pdf)
- Berliner (1994) documents musicians building "vocabulary" (licks and formulas) by imitation and applying it to tunes. [Ethnographic] — [Aksdal on Berliner](https://erlingak.folk.ntnu.no/LearningJazzLanguage/LearningJazzLanguage.pdf)

### Inferences
- Learn each standard's changes as a sequence of chunks ("ii–V to F, ii–V to Eb, turnaround") rather than 32 separate chords. Because ii–V–I and I–vi–ii–V recur across the repertoire, every chunk learned transfers to the next tune, which is the schema advantage.
- Build a small personal bank of patterns (one or two lines per ii–V or turnaround, ideally lifted by ear from recordings). Norgaard's dual-task result suggests this is exactly what frees attention for listening to the band.
- The referent (melody plus form) should be secure first. Improvising "off the melody" ties the knowledge base to the referent.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found that compares chunk-based (ii–V) vs chord-by-chord learning of progressions for memory speed or retention. The chunking claim rests on general expertise and chunking theory plus the jazz corpus and dual-task findings.
- Pressing's 1988 original and Berliner's text were not retrieved directly, only through secondary summaries.

## 5. How long or how many sessions it takes to memorize a tune, and maintaining a repertoire

### Takeaway
Time depends heavily on length and complexity. Experts memorizing classical works spend about 10–15 hours per minute of music, but an expert jazz pianist secured a short bebop AABA head in **about 45 minutes across two sessions 10 days apart**. Structure-cued memories can last years: Imreh recalled a large share of the Presto by writing it out 27 months later, with recall best at section starts and cue bars. Long-term retention appears to hinge on the structural and cue scaffolding, plus maintenance practice once learning is complete.

### Cited Findings
- Imreh: about 33.5 hours over 57 sessions across 10 months for the Presto (about 3.5 minutes long). The last 12 sessions were "maintenance" once learning was complete. — [Chaffin & Imreh 2002](https://musiclab.uconn.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/290/2013/10/Practicing-Perfection-Chaffin-2002.pdf)
- Classical benchmarks reported in Noice et al.: "10 hours of practice for each minute of performance" (Chaffin & Imreh 2002), and "15 hours of practice for each minute of performance" for an advanced student pianist (Lehmann & Ericsson 1998). Chaffin (2007) observed a concert pianist preparing the 1,607 notes of *Clair de Lune* in 4.75 hours. — [Noice et al. 2008](https://production.wordpress.uconn.edu/musiclab/wp-content/uploads/sites/290/2013/10/MemoryJazzMusician.pdf)
- Jazz head: the pianist learned *Funk in Deep Freeze* in two videotaped sessions **10 days apart (15 minutes and 30 minutes)**. At the end of session 2 it was "almost" done ("[I need] about another five minutes"). The authors note this was "much less" practice than in the classical studies because the piece was "shorter and technically simpler." — [Noice et al. 2008](https://production.wordpress.uconn.edu/musiclab/wp-content/uploads/sites/290/2013/10/MemoryJazzMusician.pdf)
- Long-term recall: 27 months later Imreh wrote out the score from memory. The probability of correct recall was higher at section beginnings and at bars carrying performance cues. — [Chaffin & Imreh 2002](https://musiclab.uconn.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/290/2013/10/Practicing-Perfection-Chaffin-2002.pdf)
- Ginsborg & Chaffin examined very long-term (6-year) memory for the words and melody of a song memorized by a singer. Title only: I did not read the content. — [Ginsborg & Chaffin 2009 ICoMS PDF](https://musiclab.uconn.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/290/2021/09/2009-ICoMS-Ginsborg-Chaffin-VeryLongTermMemoryWordMelody6Years.pdf)
- Ginsborg's anecdote: after replaying a recording many times, a *wrong* version began to sound right, so she switched to another recording. She also believes harder-to-learn music "sticks for longer", but presents no data. [Anecdote] — [Bulletproof Musician: Ginsborg](https://bulletproofmusician.com/jane-ginsborg-on-building-stronger-and-more-reliable-musical-memory/)
- Ginsborg's 60-singer study gave participants **25 minutes** to learn a new folk song, which indicates that a short song can be substantially learned in one session by experienced musicians. — [Bulletproof Musician: Ginsborg](https://bulletproofmusician.com/jane-ginsborg-on-building-stronger-and-more-reliable-musical-memory/)

### Inferences
- For an advanced-beginner amateur, a realistic expectation for a 32-bar standard's head is **several short sessions over one to two weeks**, and longer for the changes. This extrapolates from the expert figure (about 45 minutes over 2 sessions) and is not a measured figure for amateurs.
- Repertoire maintenance: once a tune is secure, brief periodic memory run-throughs (head plus changes from memory) work like Imreh's maintenance phase and an expanding-interval review. Practising re-entry at section starts maintains the structural cues that predicted 27-month recall.
- Check against more than one recording, to avoid "learning in" a wrong version.

### Gaps
- No study found that measures memorization time for jazz standards in amateurs, or forgetting curves for a gigging repertoire.
- No study found on the optimal review interval for maintaining 50+ tunes.

## 6. Singing as a learning aid

### Takeaway
Singing (and solfège or audiation) is consistently linked to ear-playing and pitch accuracy in correlational data. In the one controlled trial found, **singing alone did not beat no practice** on short-term performance, but **singing combined with playing and imagery** gave the best expressiveness. Use singing as an audiation and memory layer on top of playing, not as a substitute.

### Cited Findings
- Steenstrup et al. (2021): the singing-only condition did not significantly improve performance vs no practice, while the combined sing + play + imagine condition improved overall performance and was the **only** strategy that improved musical expression. Prior solfège training correlated with fewer pitch errors. — [Steenstrup et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.757052/full)
- Gold-MSI singing-ability subscales predicted playing-by-ear skill in a replication of McPherson's skills model. — [Melbourne repository](https://minerva-access.unimelb.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/9cc8eee9-b78d-500a-b164-828edb9384ed/content)
- Woody: ear playing depends on "inner hearing". Singing externalizes and checks that inner hearing. — [Corcoran & Spiro 2021](https://researchonline.rcm.ac.uk/id/eprint/1919/1/Corcoran%20%26%20Spiro%202021.pdf)
- In Ginsborg's 60-singer study, experienced singers learned words and music best **together**, and words and melody were remembered in association with each other (Ginsborg & Sloboda). For an instrumentalist, the lyric of a standard can serve as an extra associative cue for the melody. This is an inference from singer studies. — [Bulletproof Musician: Ginsborg](https://bulletproofmusician.com/jane-ginsborg-on-building-stronger-and-more-reliable-musical-memory/); [IJRCS Timoshenko-Nilsson review](https://acda.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/IJRCSVol13TimoshenkoNilsson.pdf)
- Berliner reports teachers "shadowing" students by singing or doubling phrases. This is a forum paraphrase and has not been verified. [Anecdote] — [jazzguitar.be forum](https://www.jazzguitar.be/forum/ear-training-transcribing-reading/81622-transcription-ear-training-drop-ocean.html)

### Inferences
- Practical sequence per tune: listen, sing the melody (with lyrics if known), sing the roots or bass line of the changes, play by ear, check the chart, then play from memory. This combines the elements the evidence links to ear-playing and the "combined" condition that worked best.
- Singing chord roots along with the recording is a low-cost way to encode the harmonic road-map aurally as well as conceptually.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found specifically on singing jazz heads or bass lines to aid memorization of changes. The recommendation is extrapolated.
- No study found that tests whether learning a standard's lyrics improves an instrumentalist's melody recall.

## Bottom line for an adult amateur tenor player (synthesis; strength in brackets)
1. **Learn the form and changes as structure with named chunks** (AABA, ii–V's, turnarounds, the A1/A2 switch points). [Moderate: Chaffin, Ginsborg, Noice jazz case]
2. **Learn melodies by ear first, then check the chart.** Ear-playing is the hub skill for improvising and memory. [Moderate, correlational: McPherson; Woody & Lehmann]
3. **Test from memory early and often, and re-enter at section starts.** This builds content-addressable memory, not just a fragile serial chain. [Moderate: case studies, Ginsborg]
4. **Spread a tune over several short sessions, interleave two or three tunes, and learn new material before sleep without stacking a second new melody straight after.** [Sleep: Moderate–Strong. Interleaving: Weak–Moderate. Spacing in music: mixed]
5. **Add singing and mental rehearsal on top of playing.** Combined practice beat playing alone for expressiveness. [Moderate: single RCT-style study]
6. **Build a personal pattern bank for common progressions.** Experts' solos are largely recombined patterns, and patterns free attention. [Moderate: Norgaard corpus and dual-task]
7. **Maintain repertoire with periodic run-throughs from memory.** Structure-cued memory can last years. [Weak/Case]
