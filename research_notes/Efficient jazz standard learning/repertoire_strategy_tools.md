# Building a Jazz-Standards Repertoire Efficiently: Tune Order, Tools, Maintenance

Audience context (from the assignment, not a source): adult advanced-beginner tenor player, weekly amateur band reading from the *557 Jazz Standards* fake book. Currently on Autumn Leaves; next probably Blue Bossa or All of Me.

## 1. "First standards" lists, orderings, and grouping by shared form or progression

### Takeaway
Educator lists and real jam call data agree on a small core: Autumn Leaves, All of Me, Blue Bossa, plus easy blues heads (C Jam Blues, Bag's Groove, Blue Monk, Billie's Bounce, Mr. P.C., All Blues) and a few 16-bar Latin or modal tunes (St. Thomas, Summertime, Song for My Father, Work Song). Autumn Leaves, then Blue Bossa, then All of Me fits both the beginner lists and the most-called data. Grouping tunes by form (blues, 16-bar minor or Latin, AABA, rhythm changes) multiplies the payoff, because most standards are built from the same blocks: major and minor ii-V-Is and turnarounds.

### Cited Findings
- **Jam-session call data (London, 2019–2021):** one musician logged 2,038 calls of 308 songs at 160 sessions, gigs and rehearsals.
  - Top 10 songs = 19% of calls. Top 25 = 36%. The site's 201 standards = about 78%.
  - The top 25 (alphabetical): All Blues, All of Me, Autumn Leaves, Billie's Bounce, Black Orpheus, Blue Bossa, Blue Monk, Caravan, Cherokee, Corcovado, Four, It Could Happen To You, Mr P.C., Night In Tunisia, On Green Dolphin Street, On The Sunny Side Of The Street, Ornithology, Recordame, Softly As In A Morning Sunrise, Solar, St Thomas, Take The A Train, There Will Never Be Another You, This I Dig Of You, Well You Needn't.
  - The author says Autumn Leaves and Take The A Train recently dropped out of the top 10 (replaced by Caravan and There Will Never Be Another You). He notes other scenes may differ but says the broad picture is "fairly consistent".
  - [StandardRepertoire – Top 25](https://standardrepertoire.com/pages/the-top-25-jazz-standards.html)
- **Learn Jazz Standards' "20 Easy Jazz Standards", with the reason each is easy:**
  - Autumn Leaves: 32-bar AABC on a circle of fourths, relative major/minor, ii-V-I practice.
  - Blue Bossa: 16-bar bossa with major and minor ii-V-Is and a "chromatic key change".
  - C-Jam Blues: two-note melody, the easiest on the list.
  - Doxy: Bb, all dominant chords, a contrafact of "Ja-Da".
  - Fly Me to the Moon: AABA in C, mostly diatonic.
  - Mack the Knife: four chords, Bb.
  - Lester Leaps In: simplified rhythm changes.
  - Mr. P.C.: minor blues.
  - My Little Suede Shoes: nearly all ii-V-Is in Eb.
  - St. Thomas: 16-bar, repetitive changes.
  - Summertime: 16-bar, A minor.
  - There Is No Greater Love: AABA, A in Bb, B in relative minor.
  - Watermelon Man, Work Song, Song for My Father: modal or one-tonality tunes.
  - What Is This Thing Called Love: called often.
  - The article's numbering is not a difficulty order.
  - [LJS – 20 Easy Jazz Standards](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/20-easy-jazz-standards/)
- **Beginner-list consensus across several sites:** Autumn Leaves (named most often as the first tune teachers assign), All of Me, Satin Doll, So What. One guide says the Blue Bossa head stays within the C blues scale, which makes it easy to pick up. [LJS – 50 Must-Know Standards](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/50-jazz-standards-you-need-to-know/); [Jazzfuel – 50 Essential Standards](https://jazzfuel.com/essential-jazz-standards/) (summarized from search snippets, not read in full)
- **"100 must-know tunes" consensus list (traced to HSPVA Houston and eight jazz educators):** the method is unknown; the top score is Take the A Train at 679. A commenter says inclusion meant "you'd be embarrassed not to know it when called at a jam". Treat it as weakly sourced. [Neff Music](https://neffmusic.com/blog/2017/05/100-must-know-tune-consensus-list)
- **How many tunes a jam actually uses:** a pool of about 30–50 commonly called tunes. What gets called "really depends on the location", and one Norwegian session called mostly "easy Real Book 1 tunes". [Jazz Guitar Online forum – most often called tunes](https://www.jazzguitar.be/forum/bandstand/13133-most-often-called-tunes-jazz-jams.html) (from snippets)
- **Shared building blocks:** Jazzadvice says most standards use major and minor tonalities, major and minor ii-Vs and turnarounds. It recommends building language for each block and comparing tunes for shared melody, chord types, progressions and form. Blues, rhythm changes and All the Things You Are are tunes "you never finish studying". [Jazzadvice – A Blueprint for Building Your Repertoire](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/a-blueprint-for-building-your-repertoire/)
- **Memorize the changes by category:** LJS advises "categorizing each set of changes rather than thinking of chords individually". Jazzadvice notes standards "are more similar than is initially apparent". [Jazzadvice – How to Learn a New Jazz Tune Every Day](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/how-to-learn-a-new-jazz-tune-every-day/) (snippet)
- **Contrafacts (a new melody over an existing tune's chords):**
  - Rhythm-changes heads include Anthropology, Moose the Mooch, Steeplechase, Shaw 'Nuff, 52nd Street Theme, Ah-Leu-Cha, Cottontail, Salt Peanuts, and (simplified) Lester Leaps In.
  - Donna Lee is built on Indiana; Ablution on All the Things You Are; Satellite on How High the Moon; Countdown on Tune Up.
  - Contrafacts are often reharmonized, so the changes may not match the parent tune exactly.
  - [Wikipedia – Rhythm changes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhythm_changes); [Wikipedia – Contrafact](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contrafact); [The Jazz Piano Site – Contrafacts](https://www.thejazzpianosite.com/jazz-piano-lessons/jazz-chord-progressions/contrafacts/)
- **Jazz-blues heads as a family:** 50 blues heads are listed in one place. One source names C Jam Blues as the usual icebreaker tune for newcomers and Now's the Time as a common blues call. [JazzGuitarLessons – Top 50 Blues Heads](https://learn.jazzguitarlessons.net/blog/jazz-blues-songs-list-top-50-blues-heads); [JazzGuitarLessons – jam etiquette and common tunes](https://learn.jazzguitarlessons.net/blog/jazz-jam-session-and-common-tunes)

### Inferences
- **Autumn Leaves → Blue Bossa → All of Me is a sound sequence.** All three are on both the "easy" lists and the top-25 call data.
  - Blue Bossa reuses the minor ii-V-i from Autumn Leaves in a short 16-bar form.
  - All of Me adds the major-key, secondary-dominant, 32-bar ABAC type.
- **Suggested family clusters after those three:**
  - (a) Blues family: C Jam Blues → Bag's Groove / Billie's Bounce / Blue Monk → Mr. P.C. (minor blues) → All Blues (6/4 modal blues). One 12-bar form serves many heads.
  - (b) 16-bar minor and Latin family: Summertime, St. Thomas, Song for My Father, Black Orpheus, Recordame.
  - (c) AABA ii-V tunes: Fly Me to the Moon, There Is No Greater Love, There Will Never Be Another You, Satin Doll.
  - (d) Later: rhythm changes (start with Lester Leaps In or Doxy-adjacent tunes), then Oleo or Anthropology.
- **For a band that reads from the 557,** the band's own call list matters more than any global list. The London data shows that the top 25 tunes cover only about a third of calls, so a band-specific list is the most efficient guide.

### Gaps
- iReal Pro has no public "most downloaded / most played tunes" statistic. I found none.
- I did not access the LJS Inner Circle or Jazzadvice PRO curricula, which are paywalled, so their exact tune orderings are unverified.
- The Neff/HSPVA list's ranking method is unknown.
- I found no peer-reviewed or multi-city jam-call dataset. The London log is a single musician's record.

## 2. Pacing: tunes per month, depth vs breadth, tune-of-the-month programs

### Takeaway
No authoritative number exists. Educators lean strongly toward depth, often one tune per month done thoroughly (melody, changes, one improvisation focus), with stretch plans of up to about 4 per month. Amateur players often carry 10–20 tunes. For a weekly band, 1–2 new tunes a month plus maintenance is consistent with every source found.

### Cited Findings
- **Depth over breadth (LJS):**
  - Repeating one standard beats learning many "that don't ever really stick".
  - The LJS podcast's "One Jazz Standard Strategy" gives a whole month to one standard: melody, chords and one area of improvement.
  - An LJS monthly-plan example lists "4 new jazz standards for the month" as a goal alongside solos and licks.
  - [LJS Podcast – The One Jazz Standard Strategy](https://metacast.app/podcast/learn-jazz-standards-podcast/D6LstnmH/the-one-jazz-standard-strategy-for-jazz/PLIzqXRE); [LJS – 4 Keys to Jazz Improvement](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/blog/learning-jazz/jazz-advice/4-keys-jazz-improvement/) (search snippets)
- **The 100-tunes-in-a-year experiment:** LJS's founder says he learned 100 standards in one year but "wouldn't necessarily advocate learning that many… so quickly" to students. [LJS Podcast – Lessons From Learning 100 Jazz Standards in a Year](https://metacast.app/podcast/learn-jazz-standards-podcast/D6LstnmH/lessons-from-learning-100-jazz-standards-in-a-year/6tQnuhFP) (episode description only)
- **Jazzadvice on pace:**
  - Cramming and deadline lists don't work. Work through the list one tune at a time rather than ten at once, with daily dedicated time.
  - Meaningful progress on the core repertoire is possible in about a year.
  - [Jazzadvice – Blueprint](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/a-blueprint-for-building-your-repertoire/)
- **Amateur forum data points (anecdotal):** about a week to get a tune's melody and changes "passable for a jam", depending on difficulty. "Many of the average players and amateurs I know play maybe 10–20 tunes." [Jazz Guitar Online forum – strategy for learning standards](https://www.jazzguitar.be/forum/getting-started/81792-strategy-learning-jazz-standards.html) (snippets)

### Inferences
- **A realistic cadence:** about 1 new tune every 2–4 weeks, learned to "can play the head from memory and follow the form". The rest of the time goes to reviewing the band's current list.
  - That gives roughly 12–20 solid tunes in a year, which matches the typical amateur repertoire above.
- **"Tune of the month" fits the user's simple-plan preference better than "tune of the week".** A tune of the week is closer to the Jazzadvice daily-learning model meant for more advanced players.

### Gaps
- I did not access the details of the LJS Inner Circle "monthly tune" program (paywalled).
- I found no data on retention as a function of pace.

## 3. Tools and how to use each for learning a tune

### Takeaway
Use recordings plus a slow-downer to learn the melody by ear (the core method). Use iReal Pro for the form and changes: backing, looping, auto-transpose, tempo ramps, Bb charts. Use stem separation (Moises, or Demucs, which the user already runs) to isolate bass or remove the horn from a recording. All sources warn that iReal and the fake book become a crutch if used in place of the ear and real players.

### Cited Findings
- **iReal Pro features:**
  - Adjustable tempo; loop any span of 2+ bars (tap-hold-drag on iOS, tap-hold first and last bars on Android, click-drag on Mac).
  - Transpose to any key. Global Eb/Bb/F/G transposition for horn players.
  - Community chart downloads and a chart editor.
  - Automatic key change at every repeat and automatic tempo increase at every repeat, which can be combined.
  - A mixer to mute or solo instruments, a hidden position marker, and a time-signature change option.
  - [iReal Pro FAQ – Practicing tips](https://technimo.helpshift.com/hc/en/3-ireal-pro/faq/326-practicing-tips); [App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/298206806)
- **LJS on iReal Pro:**
  - Helps with: a quick roadmap for unfamiliar tunes at jams; a baseline "standard" set of changes; hearing the harmony slowly; sharing charts; looping sections and testing substitutions.
  - Doesn't help when: it becomes a crutch; you read the screen instead of listening; it replaces real musicians (MIDI lacks feel and interplay); learning other styles (it sounds robotic).
  - [LJS – 5 Ways iReal Pro Will Help You (And 4 Ways It Won't)](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/?p=184687)
- **Slow-down and transcription apps:**
  - Transcribe!: the deepest desktop tool; not on phones or tablets.
  - Amazing Slow Downer: simple, cross-platform, one-time purchase.
  - Anytune: strong on mobile; adds instrument isolation; free tier is functional; jazz users isolate the bass to hear the changes.
  - Moises: AI stem separation (mute/solo vocals, drums, bass), speed change, chord detection; outputs audio, not notation.
  - AudioStretch: cheap mobile option with a spectrum view.
  - Mostly vendor blogs and forum or App Store opinions; no head-to-head test.
  - [Songscription – best apps to slow down music](https://www.songscription.ai/blog/best-apps-to-slow-down-music) (vendor); [Jazz Guitar Online – transcribing software 2023](https://www.jazzguitar.be/forum/ear-training-transcribing-reading/96036-transcribing-software-2023-a.html); [PianoGroove – Anytune Pro](https://community.pianogroove.com/t/anytune-pro-app/2576)
- **Ear-first method:**
  1. Listen "many, many times".
  2. Learn melody and chords from several recordings.
  3. Check against a fake book afterward ("Just because it's on a piece of paper doesn't mean it's correct").
  4. Sing the melody before playing it.
  - Jazzadvice: memorizing from a Real Book "takes your ears completely out of the equation".
  - [LJS – How to learn a jazz standard and not forget it](https://learnjazzstandards.com/blog/how-to-learn-a-jazz-standard-and-not-forget-it); [Jazzadvice – How to Learn a New Jazz Tune Every Day](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/how-to-learn-a-new-jazz-tune-every-day/)

### Inferences
- **Suggested tool workflow per tune:**
  1. Make a YouTube or Spotify playlist of 3–5 versions, including one vocal.
  2. Slow the clearest version down (Anytune, Amazing Slow Downer, or Transcribe!) and learn the head by ear.
  3. Check it against the 557 Bb chart.
  4. Use iReal Pro (Bb transposition on) to loop the form and play the head, then roots and guide tones, at a slow tempo with auto tempo-increase.
  5. Rehearse with the band.
  - Stem separation is useful for hearing the bass line, or for making a minus-horn track from a favorite recording. The user's Demucs pipeline already does this.

### Gaps
- **Aebersold and Band-in-a-Box were not researched in this pass.** I collected no sourced findings on how to use them versus iReal Pro.
  - Common practitioner view, unsourced here: Aebersold has real-rhythm-section feel, while BIAB and iReal are flexible but MIDI.
- I found no independent, current pricing or feature comparison of the slow-downer apps.

## 4. Repertoire maintenance: tune lists, rotation and spaced review, keys, playing with others

### Takeaway
The main retention method every source names is playing tunes regularly, ideally with other people. A written tune list with a "last played" date, plus increasing-interval review (next day, 2 days, then longer), keeps tunes from slipping. Forgetting becomes a noticeable problem around 100 tunes. Maintenance can be limited to melody plus changes.

### Cited Findings
- **LJS:** the main retention advice is "Play it all of the time": call it at jams and gigs and practice it at home. "Repetition and experience is key." Choose tunes you like, because they are easier to retain. [LJS – How to learn a jazz standard and not forget it](https://learnjazzstandards.com/blog/how-to-learn-a-jazz-standard-and-not-forget-it)
- **Jazztunes (a free tune-tracking tool):**
  - Its author: "as my repertoire approached the 100-tune threshold, I had difficulty keeping them all straight." Each new tune pushed another out of rotation.
  - The tool records when each tune was last played so you can see "which ones you need to revisit". Tunes "have to be regularly integrated into practice so that they enter long-term memory."
  - [Why jazztunes?](https://jazztunes.readthedocs.io/en/dev/about/whyjazztunes.html)
- **A spaced-review recipe (Irish-fiddle teacher, general to by-ear tunes):** once you can play a tune by ear, play it 3–4 times, replay it at the end of the session, then test it the next day and two days later. Keep going at increasing, loose intervals. [Hannah Harris – A scientific way to remember more tunes](https://hannah-harris-ceol.kit.com/posts/a-scientific-way-to-remember-more-tunes)
- **What to review (forum opinion):** melody and changes are the core "framework". Beyond that, review only what gives you the most trouble, unless a gig is coming up. A fixed schedule beats ad hoc "from time to time" review. [PianoGroove – Keeping up your repertoire](https://community.pianogroove.com/t/keeping-up-your-repertoire/8577)
- **You're never done with a tune:** Jazzadvice says to revisit tunes as harmony, technique and ear improve. [Jazzadvice – Blueprint](https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/a-blueprint-for-building-your-repertoire/)
- **Other keys:** iReal Pro can change key on every repeat, which tests whether you know a tune by ear and function or only by fingering. [iReal Pro FAQ](https://technimo.helpshift.com/hc/en/3-ireal-pro/faq/326-practicing-tips)

### Inferences
- **For this user, a simple system:**
  - One list (it could live in the study md) of band tunes with three states: learning / can play head from memory / solid.
  - Each practice session, play 1–2 "solid" tunes through once from memory, picking whichever was played longest ago.
  - The weekly band supplies natural spaced repetition for its set list.
  - Other-key practice is optional at this level. Start with a whole-step or fourth shift on easy tunes only, as an ear check.

### Gaps
- I found no jazz-specific validated review interval.
- I found no data on whether all-12-keys practice improves retention for amateurs. Advice is opinion-based.

## 5. Horn-specific issues: transposition (tenor Bb), concert fake books, playing the melody convincingly

### Takeaway
Tenor reads a whole step above concert pitch (add two sharps or remove two flats to the key signature), and chord symbols must be transposed too. Use a Bb edition or iReal's Bb setting, but talk to the band in concert keys. For convincing melodies, learn the lyrics and model the phrasing on singers: Sinatra for straight melodies, Ella and Billie for phrasing and embellishment. This is a habit with a well-documented lineage in tenor saxophone (Lester Young → Dexter Gordon).

### Cited Findings
- **Transposition rules:**
  - Tenor sounds a major ninth below written.
  - Concert to tenor written = up a whole step (concert C → D). Add two sharps or remove two flats.
  - Reading from a concert fake book, "just read up a major 2nd".
  - Transpose chord symbols as well as melody.
  - Use concert pitch as the common language when discussing chords and keys with the band.
  - [Jazzfuel – Saxophone Transposition Guide](https://jazzfuel.com/saxophone-transposition-guide/); [Best Saxophone Website Ever – Transposition](https://www.bestsaxophonewebsiteever.com/?p=4799); [BetterSax – How to Transpose](https://bettersax.com/transpose-guide-saxophones/)
  - Caveat: one BetterSax PDF misstates the octave displacement. The major-ninth figure is consistent across the other sources.
- **iReal Pro transposition:** global Bb transposition displays every chart in tenor key. [App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/298206806)
- **Lyrics:**
  - Lester Young taught that you should know the lyrics of any ballad you play.
  - Dexter Gordon often recited the first lines (sometimes the first eight bars) of a ballad's lyric before counting off. He said knowing the words gave insight into the tune's construction and helped him communicate.
  - [Wikipedia – Dexter Gordon](https://www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Dexter_Gordon); [Jazz Profiles – To Lester from Dexter](https://jazzprofiles.blogspot.com/2012/05/to-lester-from-dexter-with-cheese-cake.html) (search-snippet level)
  - LJS has an article arguing horn players should learn standards' lyrics. [LJS – Why You Should Learn The Lyrics to Jazz Standards](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/?p=37824) (title and snippet only, not read in full)
- **Which vocal recordings to use:**
  - Sinatra almost always sings the melody straight, so he is a good model for an accurate melody. Billie Holiday alters it heavily.
  - A university audition list advises learning standards from Ella Fitzgerald or Sinatra because singers sing relatively straightforward lines.
  - Bebop heads usually have no lyrics, so use horn recordings for those.
  - [University of Utah jazz tune list](https://music.utah.edu/documents/ensemble-audition-excerpts/jazz/2022/university_of_utah_jazz_tune_list_for_auditions.pdf); [LJS – How To Learn The Melody Of A Jazz Standard By Ear](https://www.learnjazzstandards.com/?p=23884) (search snippets)

### Inferences
- **Autumn Leaves and All of Me have well-known vocal versions.** Learning the head partly from a straight vocal version (e.g. Sinatra for All of Me), then a tenor version, gives both accuracy and horn phrasing.
- **Blue Bossa is an instrumental** (Kenny Dorham, trumpet). Learn it from horn recordings, such as Joe Henderson's *Page One*, which has Dorham on trumpet.
- **Since the band reads from the 557,** keep the single-tune Bb lead sheets (already the user's practice) and call keys in concert at rehearsal.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch and verify the full text of the LJS lyrics article or the Jazzadvice melody articles. Claims about them come from snippets.
- I found no source specifically on tenor players reading from concert-pitch fake books in real time at a jam, beyond the "read up a major 2nd" tip.
